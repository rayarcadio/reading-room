Sol Maria Casador kept the car idling at the curb long after the doors of North Bergen High School had swallowed her children.

She watched Rosie's ponytail bounce once, twice, before it disappeared into the crowd — chin up, shoulders back, already performing the confidence she'd need for senior year. She watched Roman a half-step behind her, backpack riding low. He glanced back once — not scared, just checking, making sure she was still there — and what he saw this time was his mother already dialing, phone rising to her ear before he'd even reached the doors. He didn't think much of it. Parents were always on their phones. He turned, and let the building take him.

She was still listening to it ring when the horn sounded behind her — sharp, unapologetic, someone back in the lane who'd run out of patience. She eased off the brake with the phone still pressed to her ear and pulled around the loop, out past the flagpole, onto the side street where nobody would honk her out of a parking spot, and cut the engine, and kept listening to it ring.

It kept ringing. She didn't hang up. She didn't speak yet, either — the line hadn't picked up, and some small, cowardly part of her was grateful for every additional second it didn't.

Fourteen years is a long time to carry a number in your contacts under a fake name and never once say it out loud to anyone who loved you.

*Fourteen years earlier*

The car was still four blocks from the address when the argument started.

"—that is not how you open with a widow, Marcus, Jesus—"

"I open with what gets results, Naomi. We are not her grief counselors. We're here to secure consent, and consent is the deliverable—"

"She just buried an empty casket—"

"She buried a flag. There's a difference, and it's the difference that matters to us."

Reyes's hand had come up flat against his chest at some point in the last half mile, not quite a shove, more a physical *stop*, and Ferrer let the car slow under it without seeming to notice he had. Whatever version of his face had been arguing with her — jaw tight, eyes flat, a man reciting a metric — folded itself away entirely by the time he pulled to the curb. It cost him nothing to do it. By the time he cut the engine, a different man was behind the wheel.

Reyes exhaled once, hard, through her nose, and reached for the folder on the dash. "Fine. Your way. But I'm doing the talking first."

"You always do." He was already smiling by the time he opened his door — warm, unhurried, a man arriving with condolences rather than a proposal — and neither of them said another word about it as they crossed the street.

Sol Maria had learned to hate one specific knock: Any knock. Any two shapes standing close together on the other side of the frosted glass, backlit, patient — the same two-person, dress-blues stillness the Marines had stood in four months earlier, telling her in careful, rehearsed sentences that Joseph Casador was missing and presumed unrecoverable, and that the United States government was very sorry.

So when she looked through the peephole and saw a man in a gray suit and a woman beside him, composed, patient, that same still, backlit shape she'd learned to hate — her whole body went cold before her mind caught up to why.

She opened the door anyway.

"Mrs. Casador." Warm and easy — the tone of a man arriving with condolences, not questions. "I'm so sorry to intrude. I'm Dr. Ferrer — I worked with your husband's unit. This is my colleague, Dr. Reyes. Do you have a few minutes?"

She should have said no. The word sat right there, ready, and she didn't say it.

But she was twenty-six and four months pregnant and completely alone in a country her husband had died for without her even being sure yet that he was dead, and a man had just told her he'd known Joseph, had *worked with him* — and there is no version of that grief where you close the door on the one person offering you a few more minutes of a man who no longer exists anywhere except in other people's memories.

"Of course," she said, and stepped back to let them in.

Reyes went first, and touched Sol Maria's arm on the way past — brief, warm, genuinely apologetic. Ferrer came in behind her, already scanning the apartment: exits, photographs on the wall, the crib flat-packed and leaning unassembled against the hallway closet.

They sat. Reyes did most of the talking, at first, and did it kindly — asked about the pregnancy, asked how Sol Maria was sleeping, asked if she had family nearby, and seemed to actually wait for the answers. It was Ferrer who eventually set a folder on the coffee table, unopened, and got to it.

"Joseph's unit was testing something," he said. "An experimental treatment. For soldiers with catastrophic injuries — regenerative medicine, years ahead of anything public. I won't pretend to explain the science to you today, because most days I can't explain it to myself. What I can tell you is that nobody yet knows what a treatment like that might do, downstream. Genetically. And your husband conceived a child during the treatment window."

Sol Maria's hand went flat over her stomach before she decided to move it.

"We're not telling you anything is wrong," Reyes said quickly, leaning forward, cutting a look at Ferrer. Sol Maria saw it. She didn't know yet what it meant. "We have no reason to believe there's any risk at all. This is caution. Just caution."

"What we'd like to do," Ferrer said, "is monitor the pregnancy. Full obstetric care, at no cost to you, with specialists who understand exactly what to look for. And — this part isn't conditional on anything, Mrs. Casador, I want to be clear — full health coverage for your family, for as long as you need it. It's the least the program owes Joseph's son."

*Son.* She hadn't told them yet. She hadn't told anyone yet, not really, the ultrasound tech's guess still unofficial, unconfirmed, sitting in her own chest, a secret she was hoarding for the baby's own christening. Ferrer stated it flatly, a fact he already owned.

She should have asked how he knew.

She asked instead, quietly, "Is there something wrong with my husband's treatment?"

"No," Ferrer said, fast and certain, and Reyes's silence sat half a second too long beside his answer. "We just don't leave anything to chance. Not with what Joseph gave for this."

Though she hadn't said yes, the refusal she wished to utter withered in her mouth, too heavy to ever find its voice.

"There's one condition," Ferrer said, and for just a moment something in his voice went precise, administrative — the warmth still there, but thinner, a coat of paint over something harder. "Everything about this arrangement stays between us. Not Joseph's mother. Not your closest friend. Not Roman himself, when he's old enough to ask. The program depends on a clean data set — people behave differently the moment they know they're being watched. I need you to understand that isn't a suggestion."

Sol Maria glanced at Reyes — this stranger who, unknown to her, had held Ferrer back on a sidewalk four blocks from here — for something. Confirmation. Warmth, maybe. Reyes gave her a small, sympathetic bow of the head, as if to say *I know. I'm sorry. Sign it anyway.*

It was free prenatal care from a specialist who knew things about her husband no one else would ever tell her, and it was health insurance for a widow with no job and a baby coming, and it cost her — as far as she could see that afternoon, signing a form Ferrer slid across the coffee table without quite letting her finish reading it — nothing at all.

She thought, faintly, that Joseph would have wanted her to take care of herself. She let that thought do more work than it should have.

She signed.

---

Roman grew in fits and starts — in a rush, then not at all, then in a rush again — the ordinary rhythm of a body with nothing visibly wrong with it.

He drew before he could write his own name — crayon fists on the backs of unpaid bills, on the margins of Sol Maria's grocery lists, once, memorably, on the inside of the closet door in a swirl of blue that took her three coats of primer to bury. By six he'd graduated to a battered spiral notebook he refused to be parted from, even at the dinner table, even in the bath, propped soggy-cornered on the edge of the tub while he sketched whatever cartoon had most recently ended. By eight it was Batman, exclusively, obsessively — page after page of the cape rendered a dozen different ways until he found an angle that satisfied him. By ten he had opinions about pencil weight. Rosie, fourteen and already fiercely proprietary about her little brother, hung his drawings on the refrigerator, swapping them out for new ones as fast as he drew them, and defended his talent to anyone who dared question it.

He had friends, loud ones, over for pizza and controllers plugged into every open port on the TV, the apartment full of the tinny gunfire of games Sol Maria didn't have the energy to police. She'd stand in the kitchen doorway sometimes and just watch him laugh at something on screen, unguarded, easy, and let herself believe for whole stretches of an evening that whatever she'd signed her name to in that folder on the coffee table was exactly what it had been sold to her as. Caution. Nothing more.

The specialists became a fact of the household — inconvenient, occasionally alarming, ultimately just weather. Down the hall, the super's elevator stuck between floors twice a month and nobody bothered reporting it anymore. Both were just something the building did now. A car service would come twice a year, then once, then some years not at all, and take Roman to an office with no sign on the door for bloodwork and a physical and a battery of questions disguised as games — *stack the blocks, follow the light, tell me what you dreamed about last night* — and Sol Maria would sit in a waiting room that always smelled faintly of new carpet and answer her own set of questions from whichever quiet young woman had the clipboard that year, and then they would go home, and months would pass, and she would almost forget.

Almost.

She came close to telling Carmen once. They'd known each other since Sol Maria first landed in Union City with a suitcase and no English worth trusting — two women who'd built a friendship out of proximity and necessity, kids close enough in age to grow up in and out of each other's kitchens. They were on Carmen's fire escape one July night, kids asleep, a bottle of Malta going flat between them, when Carmen asked — not for the first time, but for the first time in a voice gone soft with two-in-the-morning honesty — how Sol Maria managed. No husband, no family up here, a son and a daughter both, and somehow always caught up on the electric bill.

"The Marines," Sol Maria heard herself start, "there's a program, for families of—" and stopped, because something in Carmen's face had gone quiet and open, waiting, and because some other part of her had already surfaced, unbidden, the sound of Ferrer's voice at her kitchen table years earlier: *not even your closest friend.*

She swallowed the rest of the sentence.

"—a benefit," she finished instead. "Survivor benefits. It's more than I expected, that's all."

Carmen nodded, satisfied, and topped off both their glasses, and Sol Maria spent the rest of that night furious at herself for a lie so small it shouldn't have cost her anything, and unable to say exactly why it had.

The specialists went quiet for the better part of two years after that. No car service. No clipboard. Only a card at Christmas with Ferrer's looping signature and nothing else, which should have been a relief and instead left an unanswered question sitting in her chest.

She broke first.

She told herself it was a check-in. A courtesy. She dialed the number she'd never deleted and never saved under his real name, and when he picked up on the second ring — unhurried, unsurprised, as though he'd been waiting for her to be the one to call — she asked if everything was still all right. If they'd found anything. If she should be worried about anything at all.

"Everything's fine, Sol Maria," Ferrer said, warm as ever, and she could hear, faintly, papers moving on his end, a life going on in an office she'd never seen. "Actually — I'm glad you called. I was about to reach out myself. I think it's time we saw Roman again."

She didn't ask herself, that day, why the sound of his voice had loosened something in her chest instead of tightening it. She didn't ask herself that question, not that day.

The call came on a Wednesday in October, second grade, a teacher Sol Maria didn't recognize by voice yet — Roman was still new enough to the building that his teachers hadn't become names she kept.

"Everyone's okay," she said first, which meant, Sol Maria understood immediately, that something had happened that made *okay* worth specifying. "But I want you to hear this from me before it turns into a hallway story."

At recess, a boy in Roman's class named Kevin Dominguez had wet himself on the blacktop, and a bigger boy named Bobby Hernandez had decided that wasn't punishment enough on its own — had shoved Kevin back down onto the wet pavement when he tried to get up, laughing, doing it again when Kevin tried a second time, performing it now for the small crowd that had gathered.

Roman crossed the yard and put Bobby on the ground before the aide was even halfway there.

Not a scuffle. The teacher was careful about that word, said it wasn't what the school usually meant by *fight* — Bobby never got a hand on him. Roman had grabbed a fistful of his shirt, swept his leg out from under him in one motion, and had him pinned by the time anyone else understood what was happening, his forearm across Bobby's chest, his face so flat and certain that two different kids described it later, independently, as *scary,* which was a word that had never once been used about Roman before that Wednesday.

"Get up again," he'd told Kevin, not looking away from Bobby. "You're fine. Get up."

He let Bobby go the second the aide's hand landed on his shoulder — no extra shove, no last word, released the fistful of shirt without a flicker of resistance, already turning toward Kevin. Then he crouched down next to Kevin, who still hadn't moved, and said something the aide didn't fully catch, and walked him toward the building with a hand on his back, leaving Bobby sitting on the blacktop working out, in real time, what had just happened to him.

"I want to be honest with you," the teacher said. "Some people in this building are going to want to call this an aggression problem. I don't think that's what I watched on the playground monitor footage. I watched a boy decide something needed to stop, and stop it, faster and more completely than any adult on that yard could have. But it's going in a note either way, and I didn't want you blindsided by the word *physical altercation* showing up next to your son's name."

There was a meeting with the principal two days later — not a suspension, the man was careful to clarify, more of a conversation. Schools held these when they didn't know yet what box to put a child in. Sol Maria sat across the desk from him with her hands folded and did not apologize for her son, though some old instinct told her she was supposed to.

She asked Roman about it that night anyway, the same as always, sitting on the floor of his room while he drew.

"Were you scared?" she asked. Not *why did you do it.* She'd learned by then that question only got her the pool-party version of the truth.

He thought about it the unhurried way he thought about most things. "No," he said. "I wasn't anything. I just knew he had to stop, so I stopped him." A pause, his pencil still moving. "Then after, when Kevin was crying — I felt bad. Not for Bobby. For yelling at Kevin to get up like that. I didn't mean to sound mad at him."

"You weren't mad at him."

"I know. But I sounded like it." He looked up at her, briefly, something older than eight years old passing behind his eyes and then gone again. "I don't like when I sound like that."

She didn't have an answer for that either. She held onto it — not as data, she told herself, just as her son, the whole complicated shape of him — and said nothing to Ferrer for six months, the same private, wordless refusal as always.

It came up anyway, eventually, in the clinical language of a quarterly evaluation she didn't remember volunteering the answer to: *Has Roman ever shown a physical response to defend or protect another child?* She said yes before she'd thought about why they were asking, the way she always did by then.

He did it again at ten — smaller, quieter, no meeting with a principal that time, just something Rosie mentioned in passing at dinner, rolling her eyes with obvious pride: a girl on his bus route who got teased every day about her leg brace, and Roman had started saving her the seat next to him, loudly, every single morning, until there was no version of picking on her that didn't now also mean going through Roman first — no fight that time, just his presence being enough.

Two boys. Two different tools. Sol Maria noticed both and told herself she was only noticing her son.

---

Roman was nine the first time it happened.

Sol Maria found him on the bathroom floor at eleven at night, knees pulled to his chest, both hands clamped over his left eye, knuckles white, pressing so hard his whole face shook. He wasn't crying — that was the part that frightened her most, that he was too far inside the pain to even do that — just rocking, small sounds coming out of him that weren't words. She turned off the light because he begged her to, sat on the tile with him in the dark, and held on while it lasted. Forty minutes. Fifty. She counted every one of them on the face of her phone, the screen brightness turned all the way down so it wouldn't reach him.

By midnight it was over — completely, without residue, Roman blinking up at her afterward, disoriented, faintly embarrassed, already asking if he could still go to Jude's the next day. The pediatrician, two days later, found nothing. Suggested migraines. Some kids just got them. Nothing on the scans, nothing in the bloodwork, here's a prescription to have on hand if it happens again.

She didn't tell the pediatrician about the specialists. She told the specialists about the pediatrician.

On the phone, Ferrer’s demeanor shifted to one of clinical gravity. His questions were meticulously targeted: How long had it lasted? Which eye? How exactly did Roman describe the pain, and was there any warning? He promised to consult his team, using the well-worn phrase he favored so much these days. But the words had lost their human meaning for Sol Maria. She had long since stopped imagining actual people sitting around a table, picturing instead a cold, mechanical processor waiting for Ferrer to input the symptoms.

He called back eleven days later.

"We've cross-referenced against the full study pool," he said. "Nothing like this has come up in a single other case. I want to be straight with you — that's likely good news. Kids get headaches, Sol Maria. Migraines run in families more often than people realize. I wouldn't read anything into it."

She wanted to ask *whose family*, since Joseph had never once complained of a headache in the six years she'd known him, and she had no idea what ran in his. She didn't ask. He sounded so certain, and certainty from Ferrer had never once, in nine years, turned out to be false — as far as she knew.

It was around that same stretch of months that her mother Caridad's heart gave out for the first time in a Union City kitchen, and lived through it, and came out the other side of a triple bypass with a stack of new diagnoses stapled to the old ones — the diabetes she'd had for a decade, and now a heart that couldn't be trusted, and the beginnings of something with her memory that nobody wanted to name yet but that Sol Maria could already see coming, in the moments Caridad reached for her late husband's name and came back with someone else's.

The bills arrived on top of it — a second pain, with real numbers attached. Sol Maria did the math four different ways and it came out wrong every time.

She almost didn't ask. Some part of her called it theft, somehow, bringing her mother into an arrangement that had only ever been about Roman. But she was bone-tired, tired past what sleep could fix, and Ferrer had said *for as long as you need it* nine years ago and never once revised the offer, so on a Tuesday she asked, carefully, half-joking, already bracing to hear no — whether there was any way, any way at all, the plan might stretch to cover her mother too.

"Of course," Ferrer said, before she'd even finished the sentence. "Send me her information today. It'll be active by Friday."

It was active by Thursday.

She should have felt only relief. She did feel relief — enormous, humiliating relief. It made her cry alone in a parked car, because she didn't have to choose between her mother's cardiologist and her son's sneakers that month. But underneath the relief, small and cold and impossible to unfeel once she'd felt it, was a question she never let all the way up into words: *what kind of program spends this freely on a woman it has no reason to care about, just to keep watching a boy who's never done anything except get headaches twice a year?*

She never once said it out loud — not to Carmen, not to a priest, not to the mirror. She sat with it, and kept driving.

Roman turned twelve, and the quiet stretch ended.

The calls came weekly after that, always Ferrer himself now, never a clipboard or an assistant. The questions changed shape too — less *how's he sleeping* and more *has he mentioned anything unusual, anything at all, nosebleeds, blackouts, moments he can't account for, sudden anger, sudden focus, anything that felt out of character even for a second.* He asked it gently every time, dressed it up as ordinary pediatric concern, and every time Sol Maria answered honestly because she still believed, mostly, that honesty was what kept her son safe.

She started watching Roman constantly. Not obviously — never a hand on his forehead in front of Rosie, never a question that would make him feel strange in his own skin — but at dinner, she counted how many times he blinked. On the ride to school, she checked the rearview mirror and studied his face in the glass instead of the road behind her. When he came home moody from a bad grade or a fight with a friend, some small, ashamed part of her ran it against Ferrer's list before she let herself just be his mother about it.

She told no one. Not even Carmen, not even Rosie, who was old enough by then to have noticed something if her mother had let her.

She carried it alone, the way she'd been carrying all of it alone since a folder had appeared on her coffee table with Joseph's name inside it and a stranger's signature at the bottom of a page she'd never finished reading.

---

By the time Roman was thirteen, the weekly calls had a shape to them, almost a liturgy — Ferrer's voice steady and unhurried, a short list of questions, Sol Maria's short list of answers, both of them performing a normalcy neither one believed in anymore.

Then one Thursday he asked something new.

"Has he ever described a moment where he felt — outside himself? Watching his own hands do something before he decided to do it?"

She stood very still in her kitchen with the phone against her ear and the water still running in the sink behind her, and something in her chest went cold and specific: a diagnosis, not a feeling.

"Why would you ask me that."

"It's a standard question, Sol Maria. Part of a longer list."

"Don't do that." Her voice surprised her — sharper than she'd let it get with him in thirteen years. "Don't tell me it's standard. You don't ask standard questions. You ask exactly what you need to know and nothing else, I've watched you do it since I was pregnant. So tell me what you actually think is going to happen to my son."

There was a pause on the line, and for one unguarded second she let herself hope it meant he was deciding whether to finally tell her the truth.

"Can I come by?" he said instead. "I think this deserves more than a phone call."

He came that same evening, alone this time — no Reyes, no folder — and sat across from her at the small kitchen table where Roman did his homework, and let her ask everything. Was her son sick. Was he dying. Was there something in him that was going to hurt someone, hurt himself. Had they done this to him, whatever it was, or had Joseph.

Ferrer let her finish every question before he answered any of them. It was, she thought, one of the more effective things he'd ever done.

"I can't tell you there's nothing happening," he said finally. "I'd be lying, and you've never once let me lie to you and get away with it, so I won't start now. Something may be developing in Roman. We don't fully understand what, or when, or what it will look like when it surfaces. What I can tell you — what I would tell you even if it cost me this program — is that everything we're doing exists to make sure that when it happens, someone is there who understands it. Not the police. Not a hospital that panics and makes the wrong call in the first ten minutes. Us. People who've spent thirteen years learning exactly how to protect him."

"Protect him from what?"

"From the version of this that goes wrong because nobody was watching closely enough." He reached across the table, not quite touching her hand, close enough that she felt the warmth of it anyway. "I know what this looks like from where you're sitting. I would hate me too, some days, if I were you. But I have never once lied to you about the parts I'm allowed to tell you. And I am telling you — the single most dangerous thing for Roman right now would be for this to stop."

It wasn't an answer. He had told her almost nothing, dressed beautifully, and left her more afraid to leave than she'd been afraid to stay.

But he said it looking her directly in the eye, in her own kitchen, with real weight behind his voice, with the weight of a man who had spent thirteen years insisting he cared what happened to her son. And some starved, exhausted part of her — the part that had raised two children completely alone since she was twenty-six, that had never once had another adult sit at this table and tell her *I understand what's at stake and I'm handling it* — believed him. Not completely. Enough.

He left a little after nine. She sat at the table a long while after the door closed, running her thumb along the edge where Roman's math homework had worn a groove into the wood. She didn't call Carmen. She didn't call anyone. She told herself she was doing the right thing by her son — that a mother who really loved him would stay, would trust the man taking care of him, wouldn't make him explain himself twice.

She almost believed it.

---

The office had no name on the door, and the man behind the desk had made sure of that himself, years ago, back when he still bothered to notice things like doors.

Weiss did not look up when Ferrer came in. He had a manila folder open on the blotter, and a photograph clipped to the inside cover — a boy, maybe sixteen, caught in a school ID line with his eyes half-closed and his shoulders curled inward, taking up less space in the frame than the camera had given him.

"Holbin Field," Weiss said, by way of hello. "Talk to me."

"Quiet." Ferrer dropped into the chair across the desk without being asked. "Passive. Two disciplinary referrals in three years, both filed by other kids' parents on his behalf, not his own. School counselor flagged him twice for 'possible depressive symptoms,' closed both times, no follow-through. Documented bullying going back to middle school — most recently a classmate named Ascolese, on and off since freshman year. No priors. No red flags in the psych literature we'd actually trust. He is, by every metric we have, the single least interesting kid in that building."

"Which is why he's perfect."

"Which is why he's perfect." Ferrer smiled, the real one, the one he never once let Sol Maria see. "Nobody looks twice at Holbin Field. Nobody's going to ask why he got shoved into a locker in front of witnesses on the first day of school. It happens to him constantly. We're not creating a pattern, we're borrowing one."

Weiss turned the photograph face-down. "And the instrument."

"Julius Cade. Transferred in from Bayonne this spring, clean record, responsive to incentive, no loyalty to anyone in that building yet. He'll have Field in his sights by third period, and he's good — loud enough to draw a crowd, not so loud a teacher shuts it down in ten seconds. We need it to breathe a little. Ninety seconds, maybe two minutes, before anyone with authority gets there."

"And Casador's positioned to see it."

"Third period." Ferrer slid a second folder across the desk — a class roster, three names highlighted in the same yellow. "Field, Cade, Casador, same room, same hour, first day. Took some doing — the guidance office runs its schedules through a woman who's very susceptible to a phone call from an institution that could move a schedule without anyone asking why. She'll never know she moved three schedules eleven credits' worth of algebra to make it happen. Neither will anyone else."

Weiss studied the three names for a long moment, the particular stillness of a man doing arithmetic he didn't especially enjoy. "We're a long way past monitoring."

"We're testing a hypothesis." Ferrer said it without heat — flat, unbothered, a man reading off a five-day forecast. "Thirteen years of migraines, dormant markers, nothing we can provoke safely in a lab. We finally have a live variable and a controlled stimulus. A boy gets shoved in a hallway. If Casador's profile holds — if he's what the early behavioral work says he is — he intervenes. He de-escalates, maybe he doesn't, but either way we get eyes on threat response in real conditions for the first time in this boy's life. Nobody gets hurt that doesn't already get hurt in that hallway three times a week without us."

"And if the profile's wrong. If Casador does nothing."

"Then we've learned that too." Ferrer closed the roster folder. "Either outcome is data, Arthur—" he caught himself, huffed out something close to a laugh at his own slip, "—apologies. Long week. Either outcome is data."

Weiss didn't smile back. He reached for the photograph again, turned it face-up, looked at the boy's half-closed eyes a moment longer than the file required.

"Get her to keep him in the building," he said finally. "All day, if you can manage it without alarming her. I don't like variables we haven't sourced ourselves."

"She'll do it." Ferrer was already standing, already reaching for his coat, already becoming, by degrees, the warmer man who existed only for one woman in Union City who had never once seen him sit in a chair like this. "She always does."

Neither of them looked at the photograph again on the way out. Neither of them had any reason to wonder what Holbin Field was doing, at that same hour, in a garage six blocks from Harry Striker's house, or why.

They had, after all, done their research. They simply hadn't asked the right question.

---

The call came three nights before the first day of school, after both kids had gone to bed. Ferrer had learned that timing years ago — the one stretch of night when her attention was entirely his to have.

"I want to walk you through something," he said, "before it happens, so you're not caught off guard."

Her stomach dropped the way it always did now at that particular register in his voice — careful, rehearsed, a man reading from something he'd practiced saying kindly.

"There's likely to be an incident at school on the first day. A boy being bullied. We think Roman may be positioned to witness it. Possibly to step in."

"Why would you think that."

"Because it's what he does, Sol Maria. Second grade, the boy on the blacktop. The girl with the leg brace on the bus. You've told us yourself, over the years, more times than you probably remember telling us."

"You've been waiting for something like this to happen."

"We're anticipating a pattern you already described to us."

"No." She heard her own voice go somewhere she didn't recognize. "You're not anticipating it. You're *building* it. You said 'positioned' — you don't position someone in the path of something you're not causing. What are you actually trying to get out of him. Say the word. What's the reaction you're looking for."

There was a pause on the line, longer than his pauses usually ran.

"He turns fourteen in two days," Ferrer said finally. "Fully into puberty, by every marker we have. Based on everything we understand about the treatment your husband received, this is the point in a young man's development where something dormant could express itself for the first time. We don't think it will be dramatic, Sol Maria. If anything shows itself at all, our expectation is that it's something quiet. Something he could carry the rest of his life and barely notice. What we want — the only thing we've ever wanted — is to be watching when it happens, instead of reconstructing it after the fact from a headache you're describing to me three weeks later."

He didn't tell her the rest of it.

There was no version of *the primate* or *the mice* that calmed a mother instead of unmaking one, and he had learned, over thirteen years, exactly which truths were safe to spend. So he didn't tell her about the research population, years before Roman, years before Sol Maria — a single primate subject whose physical strength had outpaced every model they had within a week of reaching full maturity, or the several rodent subjects whose hearing had sharpened past the range their own instruments could reliably measure, all of it landing, without exception, at the precise moment each animal's body finished becoming what it was going to be. Nothing violent in either case. Nothing that had looked, on paper, like anything to fear. It was the shape he expected here too — a capability, arriving all at once, and then simply becoming part of the boy going forward. He believed that, mostly. Belief was easier when it came stitched to precedent, even precedent he had no intention of ever showing her.

"Before I forget," he said, "you remember years ago, when they first started — we told you it fit the pattern for cluster headaches. That's never changed. I just wanted you to hear it again before Friday. Whatever else this turns out to be, it was never that."

"I remember," she said quietly, and let him hear only that much.

"I want you to hear something else, too," Ferrer went on. "If this goes as we expect — if Roman does what he always does, and we get the data point we're hoping for — there's a real chance this is the last thing we ever need to actively test. The file could be shelved after this. We may not need to watch him this closely again. Possibly not at all."

She sat with that a moment, something in her chest that had been clenched for thirteen years testing, cautiously, whether it was allowed to loosen.

"And if you shelve it," she said. "The insurance. My mother's care."

"You'd keep full coverage for another ten years minimum, starting the day the file closes. That's not conditional on anything more from you. Consider it something we owe you, regardless of how this goes."

He never explained the ten-year timeline. Ten years was simply the maximum limit insurance algorithms could link to a single incident. It ensured that if anything went wrong inside Roman's body down the road, they would maintain seamless, unquestioned access to him without having to justify a single form. Gratitude had nothing to do with it.

"I need him there, Sol Maria," Ferrer said. "All day, start to finish. And there's one more thing. If he calls you — if he says he's not feeling well, if he asks you to come get him early, for any reason at all — I need you to say no."

She went very still. "Say no. To my son. If he's asking me to come get him."

"We don't expect it to come to that. Everything about this is built to go off cleanly — a shove, a few minutes, over before anyone's even reaching for a phone. But if he does call, and you go get him, we lose the window entirely, and every part of today has to happen again some other day we don't control. I need you to be the wall today. Not the door. Can you do that?"

She should have asked him what happened if he was wrong. The question sat in her throat, and she swallowed it instead.

"Okay," she said instead. "I'll make sure he's there."

She hung up the phone and sat a long while at the table in the dark, one hand flat against the wood, something in her almost daring to hope for an ending instead of just an easing — *cluster headaches, something extra, the file shelved* — three small mercies she gathered into her arms, telling herself they might actually be enough to carry her through Friday. She did not let herself think, even once, about the other boy. The one the shove was going to happen to.

---

The line clicked over, mid-ring, the small electronic hiccup of someone finally picking up.

"Sol Maria." Ferrer's voice, warm as ever, unhurried, a man expecting good news. "Everything all right?"

She looked through the windshield at nothing — the side street, a stop sign, a kid two doors down dragging a trash can to the curb, no idea what morning this actually was.

"He's inside," she said.

"Good." A pause, papers moving somewhere on his end, a life going on in an office she'd never once seen. "That's all we need this morning. I'll call you after third period."

"Dr. Ferrer."

"Yes?"

She had spent fourteen years being careful with this man. Grateful to him, afraid of him, dependent on him in ways she'd never once said out loud to anyone, not even herself, not fully, not until right now, watching the doors her son had just walked through.

"If anything happens to him," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she expected, steadier than she'd planned, "I will kill you. I don't care who you answer to, I don't care what letters are behind your name, I don't care how many years I've spent doing exactly what you asked. If anything happens to my son today, there is no version of your world that keeps you safe from me."

Silence on the line. Long enough that she almost thought he'd hung up.

"Nothing is going to happen to him," Ferrer said finally, and for the first time in fourteen years, she couldn't tell if she believed him or if she'd simply run out of other places to put the fear.

"Third period," she said. "You said you'd call."

"Third period," he agreed.

She ended the call herself this time — didn't wait for him to do it, didn't wait for the warm, practiced sign-off she'd absorbed a thousand times over fourteen years. She set the phone face-down on the passenger seat, and gripped the wheel with both hands, and did not drive away yet, because some part of her — the part that had been making this exact bargain since she was twenty-six years old and newly widowed and desperate for anyone to tell her Roman was going to be all right — still believed, or needed to believe, that staying parked outside that building a little longer might somehow keep him safer inside it.
