## I. Day one: The Ambulance

The first thing I knew was a hand.

Somebody had mine, tight, the bones pressed together hard enough to hurt, and the hand holding it had a fine, steady tremor running through it. I knew that grip before I knew anything else. Mom.

Then the rest came in pieces. A plastic cup over my nose and mouth, cold air hissing into it. A click after every hiss — hiss, click, hiss, click — from a box somewhere over my head. A white blanket pulled up to my chest and tucked so tight I couldn't move my arms under it. A ceiling too close, all white panels and steel rails, with a strip of blue-white light down the middle that hurt to look at. A siren somewhere, turned way down, more of a throb in the floor than a sound.

My head felt hollow. Scooped out and rinsed. My mouth was so dry my tongue stuck to the roof of it, and when I tried to swallow, nothing happened.

"Romy." Mom's face came down over mine. Her mascara had run and dried in two gray lines. "Romy, you hear me? You're okay. You're okay. I'm right here."

I tried to say *Ma*, and it came out a croak inside the mask.

"Don't talk. Don't talk, baby."

The paramedic was a woman at my head, moving slow, checking the line taped into my arm with two careful fingers and then checking it again. Across from me, on the jump seat, a man sat bolt upright in a dark windbreaker with yellow letters I couldn't read from that angle. His arms were folded high on his chest, and his eyes stayed on me — on my hands under the blanket, mostly. When my fingers twitched, his eyes went to them.

Mom had turned her back on him on purpose, her body between his eyes and my face, the only wall she had.

Through the little window up front I could see two more heads, both turning at once, left, then right, watching the road. The radio muttered numbers. No one in the back spoke.

I closed my eyes, and the cafeteria was there. Floor wax. Tater tots. The shooter in front of the water machine, thirty tiles away, and me looking at him. Just looking. Everything else in the room had dropped out — the tables, the screaming, the light — and there was only his face, his flat blue eyes, sharper than anything I'd ever seen in my life. Every pore. The sweat at his hairline. The little nick on his chin where he'd cut himself shaving.

Then a cut. Black. Then this ceiling.

A dream. It had to be. You don't see somebody that clear in real life; real life is blurry at the edges. That was a fever dream, the heat at the base of my skull, the heat the migraines bring, and then I'd passed out.

Except I was in an ambulance. So something had happened. My brain kept trying to set the two pieces side by side, and they wouldn't touch.

I pushed the mask down off my mouth. Mom tried to stop me, and I did it anyway.

"Rosie," I said. My voice was sandpaper. "Ma. Where's Rosie?"

Something moved across her face, fast. She fixed it. "I don't know yet, Romy. I'm gonna find out. Okay? They're gonna tell us."

"Did she text you?"

"Not yet."

"I texted her." I could see it — *Hey. I'm in the caf.* Then *Where r u.* Then nothing. Then Mom: *OMG!!!!* "She didn't answer me."

I turned my head toward the paramedic. "My sister. Rosalie Casador. She's a senior. Did you guys get her? Is she in one of these?"

The paramedic glanced at the man on the jump seat before she answered. Half a second. I caught it. "I don't have that information, honey. They're still working the scene."

"Can you call somebody?"

"Roman." Mom squeezed. "Let them work."

The rig leaned into a turn, a long one, and Mom's head came up. She looked past me at the back windows, at the street going by. "Wait. This isn't — Hackensack is the other way."

"We're going to Holy Name, ma'am," the paramedic said.

"Why? Hackensack is right there. It's closer. Why would you—"

"Orders came through, ma'am." The paramedic said it to the IV bag. "That's where they want him."

Mom looked at the man on the jump seat then, finally, turning halfway around. He looked back at her and said nothing at all. After a second she turned to me again and held my hand harder.

I let my head fall back. The light strip hummed. And then, out of nowhere, I remembered the weight.

Andrew's weight. Coming down on top of me when we ran for the door. His chest on my chest. His face so close I could count his eyelashes. The blood that came from the back of his head first, warm, spreading under my neck on the tile. His lips moving. Trying to say something. A word. My name, maybe, or his mom's, or nothing at all, just his mouth still trying after the rest of him had stopped.

He'd been looking right at me when it went out of his eyes.

That wasn't a dream. I knew it the second it came back. Dreams don't have weight.

"Andrew," I said. "Ma. Andrew."

Her face crumpled. "What about Andrew, baby?"

"He was—" and I couldn't finish, because a sound came up from somewhere under my ribs instead, one I'd never made in my life, ugly and loud, and once it started I couldn't stop it. I was crying so hard I couldn't breathe, the mask fogging where it hung at my chin, the hiss-click on the monitor going faster, Mom half on top of me now with both arms around my head, saying *Romy, Romy, Romy* into my hair.

Across from us, the man on the jump seat unfolded his arms. His right hand settled on his hip, an inch from the holster, and stayed there.

## II. Day one: Holy Name — The Receiving Bay

The ambulance backed down a ramp Sol Maria had never seen, into the dark under the building, and a steel door rolled down behind it.

She felt it close more than heard it, a shudder up through the floor of the rig. The siren cut out. The street went with it — the horns on Cedar Lane, the helicopter that had hung over them since Route 4 — sealed off all at once, and what was left was the engine ticking down and Roman breathing through the mask.

The back doors opened onto white light.

A receiving bay, poured concrete, scrubbed and empty, two lanes wide and built for six rigs at a time. Theirs was the only one. Every other rig at the school had been loading kids for Hackensack; she'd heard the paramedics shouting it across the parking lot. Only Roman's had turned the other way. The air smelled of bleach and exhaust and the cold that comes up off concrete underground. Somewhere past the inner doors she could hear the ER — a phone ringing, a monitor alarm, a man yelling for someone named Patrick — the ordinary noise of a Bergen County hospital on a bad day. It stayed on the far side of the glass, muffled to a hum.

They were waiting for him. Four people in navy scrubs stood in a line with a gurney already lowered to the height of the rig's floor. Behind them stood a tall man in a white coat over a suit and tie, a tablet held flat against his chest, and beside him two more men in dark windbreakers, the yellow letters across their backs readable now: FBI.

She read it twice. Three federal agents for one boy, when there were other children back at that school still being carried out on boards.

The agent from the jump seat climbed down first and walked straight to the man in the white coat. They didn't shake hands. The agent said something short and low, and the man nodded once, looked past him into the rig at Roman, and said, "Roman Casador."

He said it flat, a name he already had. Sol Maria hadn't given it to anyone in that ambulance.

Then the scrubs moved — fast, smooth, silent. Four sets of hands on the backboard, one count, and Roman was lifted out of the rig and onto the gurney while his hand was still in hers, and then her hand was empty. One of them unclipped the IV bag and hung it on the gurney's pole in a single motion. Another swung a monitor onto the rail. They worked around her.

"Ma'am." A woman in scrubs, older, gray at the temples, reading glasses on a beaded chain, had a hand on Sol Maria's elbow. Light. Warm. Firm. "Mrs. Casador. I'm Joanne, I'm one of the charge nurses. We're going to take very good care of him."

"I'm going with him."

"You'll be right next door to him. I promise you. We just need to run some tests first, and we need the room clear while we do it. It won't be long."

"What tests? He's—" She looked at the gurney. Roman had turned his head toward her on the pillow, eyes swollen red, the mask back over his mouth. "He's fourteen. I'm his mother. I go where he goes."

"I know you are." Joanne's hand stayed on her elbow. "And you're going to be twenty feet from him the whole time."

The gurney was already rolling. The tall man in the white coat walked beside it, reading his tablet, one hand resting on the rail near Roman's shoulder. The two agents fell in behind. At the inner doors the man held a badge to a black pad on the wall, the light on it went green, and the doors swung open onto a corridor with no patients in it. Roman's gurney went down it, and the doors swung shut.

He'd looked back at her. Right before. She held onto that.

"Come on, sweetheart," Joanne said. "Let me get you some water."

The agent from the jump seat stayed in the bay. He stood beside the rig with his hands folded in front of him and kept his eyes on the inner doors as Sol Maria passed him.

Joanne used a badge too, at a smaller door. The hallway behind it was quiet and carpeted, with a water fountain and a framed print of the Palisades. Halfway down, a door stood open on a little room with a couch, four chairs, a box of tissues, and a window onto the hallway in frosted glass. A plaque beside the door read FAMILY CONSULTATION.

"He's right there." Joanne pointed at the next door down, closed, a keypad beside the handle. "Right on the other side of this wall."

Sol Maria sat on the couch because her legs decided for her. Joanne brought a paper cup of water, set it on the side table, touched her shoulder, and went out, and the door eased shut on its closer.

She tried to make it make sense. A shooting, yes. Police, yes. But a locked bay and a doctor who already knew his name and federal agents riding with him, when every other family was sitting in a hallway at Hackensack. It was only Roman.

She thought of the phone in her hand that morning, idling at the curb outside the school, Ferrer picking up on the second ring. *He's in. He went in.* She'd told him Roman's head had been bothering him all week. She'd told him he was acting strange. And Ferrer had said what he always said: that she'd done the right thing, that today was a small thing, nothing that would touch him, and that she should go to work and stop worrying.

A small thing.

She folded her hands together in her lap, tight. He would have told her. If this had anything to do with the program, with Joseph, with any of it, Ferrer would have told her. Fourteen years, and he had never once let anything happen to her kids.

Through the wall, a machine started up, a low electric whine, and then voices, too muffled to make out. Sol Maria put her palm flat against the wall and kept it there.

## III. Day one: Holy Name — The Family Room

The room had been built to keep people calm. It was working on her, and she hated that it worked.

The lights sat deep in the ceiling, soft and even, with no glare anywhere. The floor was a faux wood that swallowed footsteps; Joanne had crossed it to the door without a sound. The air conditioning ran cold and steady, the only noise in the room besides her own breathing. A television hung on the wall, muted, on News 12: a helicopter shot of the high school, the roof, the parking lot full of red and blue light, and a line of text sliding under it — SHOOTING AT NORTH BERGEN HIGH SCHOOL — MULTIPLE INJURED. She watched the roof go by four times before she made herself look away.

The window onto the hallway was etched with big abstract leaves. People passed on the other side of it all the time. She could see their shapes go by, blurred into the leaves — a white coat, a dark suit, two windbreakers — and she couldn't make out a single face. Every few minutes, out in the hall, the keypad on Roman's door gave a motorized snap, and a shape went in, and the door shut, and the snap came again.

She called Rosie.

It rang once and went to voicemail. *Hey, it's Rosie, you know what to do.* Bright, a little bored, recorded junior year. Sol Maria hung up before the beep and called again, and got the same bright voice, and hung up again.

She texted. *Rosie answer me.* *Mama please.* *Just tell me where you are.* *Call me the second you see this.* Under each one, the little gray word that should have said Delivered stayed blank.

Then she opened Find My, because Rosie had let her keep it on after the fight about it last spring, and waited for the dot.

Rosie's name came up. Under it, where a blue dot should have been sitting somewhere, anywhere, it said *No location found.* And under that, smaller: *Last seen North Bergen High School, 10:54 AM.*

She looked at it until the screen dimmed, touched it to bring it back, and read it again.

At 10:54 she'd been at the salon, rinsing color out of Mrs. Ortega's hair.

A shape stopped outside the glass.

Tall. Narrow. A posture so straight she'd have known it across the Port Authority at rush hour. The door opened, and Dr. Ferrer stepped in, and the cold of the hallway came in with him.

He looked exactly as he always did: charcoal three-piece suit, no tie, the top button of his shirt fastened, his hair combed straight back. And the eyes — the left one ice-blue, the right one with its two pupils inside the broken iris, the one she'd spent fourteen years learning to look past.

"Sol Maria." He said her name gently, crossing to her. He didn't stand over her. He sat in the chair beside the couch, close enough that their knees almost touched, and took both her hands.

"They won't let me see him." Her voice cracked on it. "They took him through a door, and there's FBI, there's—"

"I know. I know."

"Is this—" She heard herself say it. "This morning. You said it was a small thing. You said nothing would touch him."

He held her eyes, steady. "What happened at that school today has nothing to do with anything you and I have ever talked about. Do you understand me? A boy with a gun walked into a school. That's all this is. Roman was in the wrong room." He squeezed her hands. "And the reason there are so many people around him is that I asked for them. He's in the best hands in this state. I made sure of it."

"And Rosie." It came out of her in a rush. "She won't answer. Her phone says no location, it says last seen at the school at ten fifty-four, she's—"

"Then let me find out." He said it slowly, every word set down. "Let me find out what happened. All of it. And when I know, you'll know. Everything. I promise you that."

She nodded. She couldn't stop nodding.

He stood and drew her up with him and held her. He smelled of starch and, faintly, of chalk, and his hand rested flat between her shoulder blades, steady, and for the first time since 10:54 her lungs filled all the way. She cried into his lapel, and he let her.

"Stay here," he said into her hair. "Drink your water. I'll come back to you."

He went out. Through the frosted leaves she watched his shape cross to Roman's door. The keypad snapped. The shape went in.

She sat down. On the shoulder of her blouse, where his hand had been, there was a faint white print of chalk dust, five fingers. She looked at it a long time before she brushed it away.
