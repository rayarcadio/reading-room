## I. Cold Open — Cafeteria, Lockdown

The tile under the table reeked of bleach and old milk. Roman would remember thinking that — out of everything happening, his brain had decided that was worth noticing.

Five tables between them and the hallway. They were at the third one, counting from the door. His backpack was wedged against his knee, the corner of his sketchbook digging into his leg through the canvas. He didn't move it. Moving would be the wrong kind of loud right now.

Mrs. Okafor had the lights off and her finger against her lips — the same hush she'd have used on a room of eight-year-olds during an overplayed fire drill. Nobody was laughing. A girl two tables over was praying in Spanish, so quiet it was just breath with shapes in it.

Then the shots came again. Closer.

Not fireworks. Not lockers. Roman knew the sound lockers made — he'd slammed enough of them. This was flatter. Meaner. Each one landed in his chest before it landed in his ears.

He'd already texted Rosie twice. Fingers shaking too much to type clean the first time — *Hey. I'm in the caf* — then, when nothing came back inside of a minute, a second one, faster, no punctuation: *Where r u*

He stared at the screen after that one, waiting on the three dots. Nothing. Not even the dots.

He told himself that made sense. If she was hiding good, actually hiding, actually safe, she probably had her phone silenced, face-down, tucked somewhere she couldn't reach for it without moving and giving herself away. No dots was supposed to mean safe. He decided to believe that, because the other option wasn't one he could hold onto and still function.

He switched over and texted Mom instead. *have you heard from Rosie?*

The reply came back almost instantly — Mom's typing was always instant, always too fast, her thumb basically living over the send button. *No… why? Is everything ok*

He typed *you haven't heard?* and immediately regretted it, because now she'd know something before he could explain it, before he even knew how to explain it himself.

He switched back to Rosie's thread. Still nothing. Not delivered-and-read, not even delivered for sure — the little status under his last text hadn't changed in almost two full minutes now, which for Rosie, who texted back before people finished sending, might as well have been an hour.

Then, from Mom: *OMG!!!!*

And then his phone buzzed again, different this time — not a text, the screen lighting up whole, ringing, the light bleeding through his pocket, threatening to give him away. He checked it anyway. Mom.

He answered it, barely audible. "Mom—"

"Roman, baby, are you—oh my God, oh my God, they're saying there's a shooter, they're saying North Bergen PD is already—Roman, talk to me, are you safe, where are you—"

This wasn't her voice. This was something wearing her voice.

"I'm okay," he whispered. "I'm hiding. I'm okay."

"Don't hang up. Please don't hang up, I need to hear you, I need—"

Another shot. Closer than the last one. Close enough that somebody under the next table let out a gasp with pain already built into it, even though they probably hadn't been hit at all.

The door at the end of the hallway banged once. Hard. A test of its weight.

Roman went stiller than he'd ever been. Even his thoughts stopped moving.

And he knew it with the same body-deep certainty you feel a wave coming before it reaches you — whatever was on the other side of that door was going to be standing in this room in about four seconds.

## II. Roman's Room — Morning, Day One

I woke up before my alarm, which never happens, and for a second I just laid there listening to the apartment do its thing — pipes ticking somewhere behind the wall, Mom's shower running down the hall, the traffic on Bergenline starting to wake up too. First day of freshman year. Fourteen years old exactly one day now, which still felt strange to say — the number sat on me stiff and new, not yet washed soft.

My head felt weirdly clear, too. Not groggy at all, not the usual fog I usually gotta fight through before I even want to be awake. Every little sound in the apartment seemed sharper than it should've been this early — I could actually pick out Mom humming something under the shower water, could hear the specific rattle of the radiator two rooms over, close and immediate, as if it sat right beside me. I didn't think much of it right then. I figured maybe I'd just slept good for once.

I reached over and grabbed the journal off my nightstand. The new one. Still had that stiff, unbroken spine, that smell paper gets before anybody's touched it — Danny's gift, my cousin, who always somehow finds the one thing I actually want instead of the thing everybody assumes a fourteen-year-old wants.

I flipped past the first few pages I'd already filled — a subway rat wearing a crown, half a mural idea I'd seen in my head after passing some brick wall on Kennedy, Rosie's profile from memory, the one where she's laughing at something off-page — and found a blank one. Started sketching without really deciding to. That's usually how it goes. My hand just starts moving before my brain agrees to anything.

I was drawing my own hand, actually. Just resting flat against the sheet, fingers spread, shading in the knuckle shadows using Ms. Rivera's old method from eighth grade — thumb pressure light, letting the graphite do the work instead of muscling it in. She was the first person who ever told me I was actually good at this, not just "good for a kid." I still think about that sometimes, way more than I probably should for something somebody said in grammar school.

And I stopped.

Because I was looking at my own fingers. Really looking. Really looking, because he'd just found out they weren't what he'd always thought they were.

Yesterday those fingers turned into gun metal in front of a TV screen. I watched it happen. Felt it happen — this cold, tight pulling sensation, my bones seeming to know something my brain didn't, something in them finally getting the fourteen years of permission it had been waiting on.

I flexed my hand. Normal. Just a hand. Just skin and knuckle and the little callus I've got on my middle finger from holding a pencil too hard for too many years.

But I already knew normal didn't mean safe anymore. Normal was just whatever got shown on the outside.

I put the pencil down and let my head fall back against the pillow, staring at the ceiling, and I begin to think about yesterday. Warm and fuzzy yesterday. My birthday, which landed the day before school started again — in the past it's either been the same day or the second day of school. Other kids get that thing where their moms send in cupcakes or somebody's dad shows up with a stack of pizzas fourth period and the whole class sings. But it's hard to do that at the beginning of a school year, when everyone is new and there's no class chemistry yet.

I let myself go back there.

## III. Flashback — Birthday Morning

Today started at the Beacon Diner in Fairview, same as every weekend — our booth, the one by the window that we've basically claimed as ours since I was like six. The vinyl's cracked in the same three places it's always been cracked.

A waitress came over with menus, older, maybe late sixties, hair dyed a red that doesn't occur in nature. Her name tag said DAWN. She wasn't rude or nothing, just brisk — thirty years on a diner floor will do that to a person, burn off whatever energy they used to have left over for small talk they don't actually need to make.

"Morning, Sol Maria. Drinks to start?"

"Orange juice," Rosie said.

"Just water," from Mom.

"Chocolate milk," I said, and Dawn nodded, not even blinking at a fourteen-year-old ordering it, and walked off without writing it down — didn't need to.

She came back a minute later with the drinks, set them down, pulled her pad back out. "Y'all ready, or you need a minute?"

Before we could answer, Mom reached over and put the back of her hand against my forehead, quick — an old fever-check reflex, even though this wasn't that kind of morning. "How's your head this morning? No pain, nothing?"

"I'm good, Ma. Nothing."

"Good." She said it easy, but her eyes lingered on me a second longer than the question needed. They always did, this time of year. My migraines liked to show up in the mornings, out of nowhere, and she'd been bracing for one every year since I was little, cake or no cake. "That's a first, then. Every year, right around now—"

"I know, Ma."

"I'm just saying. Maybe you outgrew it." She said it hopeful — wanting it true more than she actually believed it.

"Dr. Ferrer always said these things sometimes just age out on their own," she added, more to herself than to me. "Cluster headaches, that's what he called them. Never did explain why they mostly showed up in the mornings."

I nodded along, not really placing the name — Dr. Ferrer wasn't anybody I actually saw. My actual doctor was Dr. Mangosing, some office in Jersey City Mom liked, and I'd never once heard her mention a Ferrer before this morning. I figured maybe he was somebody from way back, before I was old enough to remember appointments, and let it go the same as everything else.

I didn't tell her the real reason I didn't have a migraine that morning was because whatever used to come out sideways, as pain, had apparently found a completely different way out of me now. I just said, "Maybe," and left it at that.

"French toast, extra banana, side of bacon — like, really crispy, almost burnt, and whip cream on the side, not on top," I said.

Dawn wrote it without looking up. "And for the ladies?"

Mom and Rosie ordered — I wasn't really looking at either of them while they did it, watching instead some guy in the next booth read an actual physical newspaper, dead serious about it, some relic out of 2005, but I heard every word anyway, clear as if they were sitting right next to my ear instead of across the table. Then Mom said she had to use the bathroom right quick, and slid out of the booth.

She didn't go far before she leaned into the counter and said something to Dawn, low, quick, meant for nobody but Dawn — except I caught every syllable of that too, easy as anything. *Can you put a candle in his plate when it comes out? It's his birthday, we don't usually make a big thing of it but I want to today.*

Dawn's eyes flicked over to me for half a second, then back to Mom, and she nodded, all business now, a woman with a job to do.

I looked back down at my menu, giving nothing away. Smiled a little into it, private, and kept it to myself. Wasn't gonna ruin Mom's moment by letting on.

A few minutes later the food came out — except it wasn't just Dawn. It was Dawn and two other waiters, one of them balancing a tray, and they were walking toward our booth at this deliberate, semi-choreographed pace, and I did the thing where you widen your eyes a little, sit up straighter, let your face go *wait, what* — because even though I already knew exactly what was about to happen, Mom didn't need to know that. Nobody did. Letting her have the surprise even though I couldn't was the least I could do.

There was a candle stuck in my French toast.

And then they started singing. Not great, not rehearsed, just loud — diner staff who've done this a thousand times and stopped caring how it sounds — and a couple people in nearby booths joined in too, this older couple by the door, a guy in scrubs eating alone, the whole thing catching mid-verse and spreading, contagious. Mom was singing the loudest, off pitch on purpose, and Rosie had her phone out recording, laughing so hard she could barely hold it steady.

I sat there playing the part, face hot for real even if the surprise wasn't, not knowing where to put my hands, grinning anyway — and somewhere under the performance of it, actually meaning the grin too. Both things at once. I was getting used to that.

"Make a wish, birthday boy," Dawn said, not smiling exactly, but not not smiling either — just satisfied, job done.

I blew out the candle. Didn't tell anybody what I wished for. Wouldn't have mattered anyway — whatever I wished for that morning, it wasn't this.

First bite of the French toast, and it hit different than French toast usually hits — the cinnamon suddenly its own separate thing instead of just part of the flavor, the banana almost too sweet, the burnt edge of the bacon distinct from the rest of it, every note of it in high definition instead of regular. I didn't think much of it in the moment. Just figured the food was good today. I didn't yet have a reason to wonder why *good* suddenly meant something different than it used to.

Rosie slid in across from me still laughing, wiping under her eye. "Happy birthday, baby brother." Then, to Mom: "He's fourteen now. Basically an adult. Watch, he's gonna start talking back."

"He already talks back," Mom said.

"Learned it from you."

Mom laughed at that — the real laugh, not the one she does for customers at the salon — and for a second it was just us, syrup smell and old coffee and somebody two booths over still humming the last few bars of the song, reluctant to let it go.

Then Mom got the look. The one that means a speech is loading.

"You know," she said, wrapping both hands around her mug, "high school is really it, Romy. This is where it starts. Whatever you wanna be — really be — these next four years decide a lot." She turned and looked at Rosie for a second, really looked, something warm and a little wistful passing over her face — she was seeing four years at once, not just the one sitting across from her. "Look at your sister. Four years ago she's sitting exactly where you're sitting, all nervous about freshman year, and now — top of her class. Rutgers wants her. Criminal justice, law, she's gonna walk into a courtroom one day and run it." She turned to me, eyes soft but locked in, love and pressure, wearing the same outfit on her face. "You're smart like that too, papi. Smarter, maybe. Doctor, lawyer, whatever — the door's wide open. I just need you to walk through it."

I nodded, because what else are you gonna do, and put a piece of French toast in my mouth so I wouldn't have to say anything back right away.

Rosie clocked it. She always clocks it. She slid around the booth without asking, right up next to me, and put her arm around my shoulder, quick and easy, no big deal made of it — even though I could feel it was, a little, in how she left it there a beat longer than a joke would've needed.

"Or," she said, easy, already helping herself to my whip cream, "he draws something one day and y'all are fighting over who gets front row at his gallery opening." She looked at me. "For real though, you should go see Mr. Meli when school starts. He's the art teacher, supposedly he's the one — Danny's friend had him last year, said he actually gets kids into real programs, portfolio stuff, scholarships even. Just show him your book. Let somebody who actually knows say if there's something there."

Mom didn't argue. Didn't really agree either. Just did that smile you do at a birthday wish you don't think is gonna come true but you're not gonna be the one to say it out loud.

I sat with both of them for a second — Mom's version, Rosie's version — two coins that couldn't both land heads, turning them over and over in my head. It wasn't that one of them was lying. That was the weird part. Mom actually believed what she said about the door being open. Rosie actually believed the gallery thing wasn't a joke. Two people who loved me, both telling me something true, and the two truths didn't fit in the same hand at the same time.

I didn't pick one. I don't know why picking felt wrong, but it did — picking a side meant deciding, somewhere underneath it, that one of them was more right than the other, and I wasn't sure that was even a thing you could decide. Maybe it wasn't about picking. Maybe you were supposed to hold both until you found whatever was actually underneath them, the real thing neither one had said outright.

That's just how I am, I guess. Slow to jump anywhere. Careful, my mom calls it — thinks about stuff too long before he moves, my dad probably would've said, if I'd ever had one around to say it. I've never once in my life just reacted to something without turning it over first, checking all the sides of it. Rosie used to tease me that I'd stand at a crosswalk doing math on a car that was clearly two full blocks away.

I didn't say anything — just *Mr. Meli*, and the two coins, and the not-picking.

## IV. Present — Roman's Room, Before School

*[I let the pencil go loose in my hand — not really drawing anything anymore, just tracing shapes that didn't mean nothing yet. Took a breath, a real one, all the way down, let it back out slow.*

*I don't think about it enough, ngl — how much of my life just runs on love that happens to come in women. Mom, up before the sun, burning her hands for me on stuff she can't even really afford most weeks. Rosie, half asleep and still finding the exact right thing to say before I even know I need it. Even yesterday — that whole diner thing, corny as it was. Rosie's arm basically wrapped around me before she even knew what she was protecting me from.*

*It's crazy. I've been getting carried this whole time by people who never once asked me to carry nothing back.*

*I don't know why that hit different right then, laying there. Maybe 'cause for the first time I had something I literally couldn't hand off to any of them. Something none of that love could reach into and fix.*

*I looked back down at the pencil, still loose between my fingers, and noticed the wood grain running along the shaft of it — not just noticed, but really saw it, every ring and fiber distinct, more photograph than tree at this range. Same with the paper under it, the tooth of it, thousands of tiny fibers catching the graphite instead of just looking smooth from a normal distance. I didn't know what that was about either, and let myself sink back into yesterday, back before any of it started — or before I'd noticed it starting, anyway.]*

## V. Flashback — Birthday, Continued

By eleven we were back at the apartment, and Mom wasted no time turning us into an errand crew. "Sazón, soda, chips, ice, cups, plates, napkins, ice cream," she said, folding five twenties into Rosie's hand. "A hundred, that should cover it. Write it down so you don't forget nothing. Fortieth Street bodega, not Broadway."

She pulled Rosie a few steps away toward the kitchen, low voice, clearly not meaning for me to hear it — except I did. All of it. Clean as if she'd said it right next to my ear instead of six feet away with a running faucet between us. "Felix is picking up the cake from Dulce de Leche, don't let him talk you into stopping anywhere else, we don't got time." Ordinary. Nothing. But I stood there a second too long, a little thrown by the fact that I'd caught every word of something that wasn't supposed to reach me at all.

"We good?" I asked, mostly just to see what Rosie would do with it.

"Nothing you need to worry about," Rosie said, way too casual, already grabbing her keys off the hook, giving me absolutely nothing to work with.

I didn't say anything else — not what they'd said, but the fact that I'd heard it at all.

The bodega on Fortieth was tight and loud, aisles barely wide enough for one person to squeeze through. Rosie peeled off toward the register with her list; I got sent to the spice shelf for the sazón — one crowded shelf, labels facing every direction, packed so tight it read less as a display than as years of accumulation nobody had ever gotten around to straightening out.

I scanned it for the yellow packet with the rooster on it.

And my eyes did something.

It wasn't squinting. It wasn't looking harder. The whole shelf just — narrowed. Everything at the edges went soft and far away all at once, and dead center, this thin bright line appeared, a crosshair, steady as anything, locked over a packet three rows back behind two cans of guandules, and the tiny print on it read as clean as if it sat six inches from my face instead of six feet. *Sazón con culantro y achiote.* I could see a tear in the corner of the packaging. A fingerprint smudge on the plastic that wasn't even mine.

I gasped, out loud, actually out loud. Some old man two feet away looked at me sideways, fully convinced I'd lost it.

I shook my head hard, and the crosshair snapped away, the whole shelf rushing back into normal blur and clutter, my heart going so fast I could feel it in my teeth.

"You good?" Rosie called, already juggling a bag of ice against her hip.

"Yeah," I said, grabbing the packet — exactly where the crosshair had already shown me it was. "I'm good."

She looked at me a second too long. Then let it go, because it was my birthday.

"Weirdo," she said, bumping my shoulder on the way to the register.

I didn't tell her. I didn't tell anybody.

I just held onto it — same as Mr. Meli's name from breakfast — another stone dropped quiet into the same pocket, getting heavier than the first one already.

There was a kid already at the counter by the time we got up there, younger, maybe eight, counting quarters out of a closed fist one at a time onto the counter, recounting the same three over and over. A bag of ice sweating a ring into the counter next to him, one Arizona can beside it.

Behind him in line, some older kid — high school, backpack still on both shoulders, phone already up and filming — went, "Yo, c'mon, you're holding up the whole line, bro," loud enough for the front of the store to hear it, timed more for the phone than for the kid.

The kid's ears went red. He recounted the same three quarters a third time.

Rosie was already setting her stuff on the counter, digging through her crossbody for Mom's twenties, not clocking any of it. I put the sazón down next to her bags and walked up beside the kid.

"How much you short?"

He didn't look up. "A dollar."

I put a dollar down on the counter before he could decide whether to be embarrassed about it, then looked past him at the guy with the phone — held his eyes, waited him out.

"You good?"

He lowered the phone, half a laugh already dying on his face, looking for somewhere else to put his eyes. "Yeah. My bad."

"All good."

By the time the guy behind the counter rang up the ice and the soda, phone-kid had found something very interesting about the energy drinks three feet to his left. The little kid grabbed his bag, gave me one look on the way out — flat, checking whether I was going to want something back for it — and pushed through the door into the afternoon.

Rosie reappeared at my elbow, finally holding actual cash. "What'd I miss?"

"Nothing."

"Mm-hm." She didn't push — just bumped my shoulder again and started unloading everything onto the counter.

It just didn't seem worth saying out loud.

## VI. Present — Roman's Room, Before School

*[Still in bed, still sketching, I heard Rosie's footsteps coming down the hall before I even heard her voice — quick, purposeful, the specific pace of somebody with a plan.*

*"USING THE BATHROOM!!" she yelled through my door, not asking, informing, already past it by the time the last word landed.*

*I let out a breath. Lost the race again, same as basically every morning of my life. Didn't really mind it though — I wasn't in a rush, wasn't even really thinking about the bathroom until she brought it up, too deep into the journal and into yesterday to care much either way.*

*What I did notice was how awake I already was. Fully awake, no fog, none of the usual dragging myself up out of sleep on a school morning. I usually needed like twenty more minutes than this before I felt human again. Today I'd been sharp since the second my eyes opened.*

*Figured it was probably just nerves. First day of school does that to people, right? Or maybe it wasn't nerves at all. Maybe it was just the other thing, the thing I didn't have a name for yet, already rearranging how my whole body worked before I even got out of bed.*

*I didn't know which one it was. I let it go, same as everything else.]*

## VII. Cafeteria — Present, Continuous

Time was doing something strange. Roman couldn't tell if it had slowed down around him or if he was the one moving through it slower, but either way the cafeteria had gone quiet — too quiet for a room this full — kids covering their ears now, the silence itself gone loud, everyone bracing without making a sound.

Except it wasn't actually silent. Not to Roman.

Underneath the held-breath quiet, he could pick out individual breathing — dozens of separate rhythms, fast and shallow, some kids holding it entirely and then releasing all at once. Whispering that sounded close, right at his ear, except when he turned his head there was nobody there — it was coming from clear across the room, from kids three and four tables away, praying in three different languages, one girl repeating the same six words over and over, rosary-steady, someone else murmuring something steady and calm, an older kid trying to talk a younger one through it in a voice built for exactly this. Fear, and prayer, and something with an edge of kindness in it somewhere, all of it arriving at once, all of it too clear to be normal.

He didn't understand it yet. Just knew, somewhere underneath the confusion, that something had actually happened to him — that this wasn't nerves or adrenaline doing something ordinary to his senses. This was different. This was new.

And watching all of it — the panic, the praying, fifty different kids handling the same fear fifty different ways — his mind went where it hadn't let itself go yet.

*Rosie.*

Where was she. What lunch did she even have today. Was she somewhere safe, crouched under some other table, this same kind of hiding, or —

*Oh God, no. Please let her be okay.*

## VIII. Present — Roman's Room, Before School

*[My bedroom, early — "Why am I feeling like this," I said out loud, to nobody, staring at the ceiling. I hadn't drank anything weird. Wasn't bit by a radioactive spider or whatever. Seriously, what the hell.*

*And seriously — what the hell was that finger thing yesterday. Andrew didn't notice, too deep in the game to notice his own hands most days. But Jude. Jude I wasn't so sure about. He'd gone quiet after, quieter than usual even for him, and he'd left kind of early, and he hadn't really looked at me right the rest of the night. Could've been nothing. Probably was nothing.*

*I didn't love how unconvinced I sounded, even just thinking it to myself.*

*I let it sit there, unresolved, and went back to yesterday anyway — because yesterday had plenty of questions too, the shelf, the finger, all of it, I wasn't pretending otherwise. It just came wrapped in a whole day of good stuff around it, cake and Rosie and the diner, enough padding that the questions hadn't found a way to actually hurt yet. Today didn't have any padding on it at all.]*

## IX. Flashback — Birthday, Continued

By the time we got back with the bags, the apartment already smelled different — Pine-Sol and something sweet underneath it, candle wax maybe, and Mom had music going low from the kitchen speaker, old stuff, the kind she plays when she's cooking and thinks nobody's really listening. The banner was up. The balloons were up. She didn't even let us react to it for more than a second before she was already directing traffic — ice in the cooler, sodas in the fridge, chips in the bowl, not the bag, Romy, we're not animals.

Jude and Andrew showed up around one, Andrew first, gripping his controller with the seriousness of a guy who'd brought his own weapon to a fight, which honestly tracked.

"Yo, happy birthday, bro," he said, doing that half-hug half-handshake thing. "You good? You look tired."

"I'm fourteen now. I age different."

"You look the same as yesterday, dumbass."

Jude showed up a few minutes later with a card he'd clearly signed in the elevator on the way up, ink still looking fresh, and we posted up in my room with the console because the living room was already getting overtaken by folding chairs and a card table Mom had dragged out of the closet. Call of Duty, obviously — same lobby we'd been running for like a year, same maps, same arguments about who camps too much.

We were maybe twenty minutes into a match, some search-and-destroy thing, three of us on a squad, when it got tight — enemy pushing our flank, Andrew yelling directions nobody was following, Jude dying immediately, same as he always does — and I lined up a shot on a guy peeking a corner, thumb over the trigger, and I felt it.

Not slow. Nothing at all similar to the shelf thing. Fast, and wrong, this hot-cold pull starting right at the base of my finger, something underneath my skin done asking permission this time.

I looked down.

My finger — my actual, real, index finger, the one on the controller — had gone this dull gunmetal gray at the tip, hardening, the nail disappearing into something smoother, something that didn't belong to a hand.

"Ah—" I yanked my hand back into my chest so fast the controller went flying off my lap, clattering onto the floor.

"Bro, what—" Andrew didn't even look over, too locked into the game. "You good?"

"Yeah — ow, my finger, I think I jammed it or something." I was already standing, cradling my hand against my stomach, actually injured or close enough to it. "I'll be right back."

"You're holding up the match," Jude said, not even glancing away from the TV.

Except he had glanced away. Just for a second, just long enough. I didn't clock it at the time — I was too busy trying not to lose my mind — but Jude had looked over right as it happened, right as my finger was still catching the last of that gray, and he didn't say anything. Not then. Not for the rest of the day, either. He stayed for the food, stayed for the cake, said all the normal stuff on his way out the door that night — "happy birthday, bro, see you tomorrow" — but something in his face had gone quiet, a stillness that didn't belong to him.

I locked myself in the bathroom to make sure I'd actually gotten away with it.

I stood there over the sink, staring at my own hand under the light, willing it back to normal with the same useless intensity you'd will a phone to charge faster. It took maybe ten seconds — ten of the longest seconds of my life up to that point — before the gray receded back into skin, the nail reappearing, everything folding itself back into ordinary human finger, no trace of it ever having gone anywhere.

I splashed water on my face. Looked at myself in the mirror longer than I needed to.

*Twice in one day,* I thought. *That's not nothing anymore. That's a pattern.*

I went back and finished the match. Died almost immediately, actually, because I couldn't stop looking at my own hand instead of the screen.

## X. Present — Roman's Room, Before School

*[My bed, I held my finger up in front of my face and just stared at it, willing it to happen again, on purpose this time, wanting hard enough to summon it, treating it as some muscle I could flex if I just found the right angle. I squeezed my eyes half shut, concentrated until my head actually started to hurt a little, imagined that same hot-cold pull from yesterday.*

*Nothing. Just a finger. Just skin and knuckle and the callus.*

*Figures,* I thought. *Works when I don't want it to. Won't work when I do.*

*I let my hand drop back onto the blanket and went back to yesterday, because at least yesterday already happened, and I already knew how to survive it.]*

## XI. Flashback — Birthday, Continued

Dinner started around five, once the actual family started rolling in — Tío Felix first, holding the cake box flat with both hands, moving it with the care you'd give something that could actually go off, then a wave of cousins, then Titi Carmen with her famous flan that nobody asked for but everybody eats. The apartment got as loud as it only gets a few times a year, everybody talking over everybody, three conversations happening in the same six feet of space.

The oxtail came out first — Mom's rabo, dark and falling-off-the-bone, the smell of it alone enough to make grown men go quiet for a second — with the rice and black beans, and I tore into it, starved, which, honestly, between the diner and the bodega and the CoD thing, I basically hadn't been able to actually taste anything until right then. Except now that I was tasting it, I was *really* tasting it — not just the usual stuff, the garlic and the cumin and whatever else she puts in there that she'll never actually tell anybody, but the individual pieces of it, my mouth suddenly able to take a dish apart ingredient by ingredient instead of just eating it. I could pick out the exact moment the sazón hit different from the jarred stuff, could taste something faintly metallic off the pot itself, some mineral note under everything else that had probably always been there and I'd just never noticed. It should've freaked me out. Mostly it just made the food taste better than it had any right to.

For a while it was just normal. Just good. Stories getting told that everybody already knew the ending of — Tío Felix telling the one about Dad's cousin and the flat tire in Atlantic City for the four hundredth time, Mom laughing at it fresh, same as she had the three hundred and ninety-nine times before.

Then the cake came out, and everybody sang, actually good this time, actually rehearsed a little because it's family, and I blew out fourteen candles plus the one for good luck, and made a wish I still didn't tell anybody, same as the diner, except this one I meant a little harder.

## XII. Present — Roman's Room, Before School

*[I'd only made it as far as the bathroom, sitting on the closed toilet lid, still half asleep, holding the bracelet in my hand instead of doing anything useful with the five minutes I had left. The little silver rose caught the light differently in here than it had the night before — sharper, somehow, more of it visible than I used to see in anything. I'd grabbed Rosie's card off my nightstand on my way in too, out of habit more than anything, and it was sitting on the edge of the sink now, still slightly bent from riding around in her back pocket all day yesterday.*

*I let myself sink back into yesterday — right after the cake, right when she'd actually handed all of this to me.]*

## XIII. Flashback — Birthday, Continued

Gifts came after. Cousins gave cousin-gifts — a hoodie, gift cards, a stuffed animal from a younger cousin who clearly still thought I was nine. Danny, my cousin, the one who actually pays attention, handed me a flat rectangular box wrapped bad on purpose, making sure I knew he'd wrapped it himself.

"You already know what it is," he said. "You've been through like three of the last one."

It was a journal. New, blank, that stiff unbroken spine, that smell paper gets before anybody's touched it. "You're welcome," he said, before I even said anything, and I laughed and told him he knew me too well, which he did.

Mom went next, sliding a garment bag across the table she'd clearly been hiding all night, waiting for the right moment. Inside was a pair of dark jeans, stiff and unworn, a Basquiat tee she'd found on some Old Navy collab rack — "so you look like an artist already, since apparently that's the plan now too," she said, half joking, eyebrow raised at Rosie, daring her to deny whose idea that comment actually was — and a fresh pair of Jordans, box still taped shut.

"For tomorrow," she said. "First day. You gotta walk in looking like you own the place."

"These are hard, Ma. Actually hard."

"I know. I have taste." She kissed the top of my head. "Happy birthday, papi."

Rosie went last. She always goes last, on purpose, because she likes the anticipation, because she's extra like that.

She handed me a small box, the kind that makes your stomach do something before you even open it because you already know it's not gonna be socks.

Inside was a bracelet, but not the kind that hangs loose or dangles anything. This was a solid silver band, hard metal, cool and heavy, with none of the flimsiness of costume jewelry — except it didn't fully close into a circle on its own. One end stopped short, and the gap wasn't a flaw, it was a *socket*, shaped to take a key. The key itself was the missing piece — not hanging off the band as a charm would, but built to slide into that open end and complete the circle, becoming the latch that held the whole thing shut. The head of the key was a rose, small and detailed, silver petals folded in on themselves, and once it clicked into place, you couldn't tell anymore where the band ended and the rose began — the whole thing just became one unbroken loop of silver, no clasp, no chain, nothing that could accidentally come undone. Running along the top of the band, above where it would sit on the wrist, was a pattern of engraved lines, vertical and diagonal, cut all the way from one end to the other — her whole Instagram distilled into metal, the rose thing she's had as her logo since freshman year, the thing on all her cheer bags and her senior shirts, turned into something you could actually lock yourself into.

"Rosie—"

"Don't talk yet, I have a whole thing," she said, already pulling a card out of her back pocket, slightly bent from sitting there all day. "Read it later, not in front of everybody, you'll get embarrassed and so will I."

I opened my mouth — actually opened it, the words already halfway up my throat, *Rosie, something's wrong with me, something's actually wrong* — and I don't know what stopped it. Maybe the noise. Maybe just not wanting to be the reason the whole room went quiet on my birthday.

"...Thanks," I said instead, and slid the open band around my wrist right there, pressing the key home until it clicked into place, closing the circle. "This is sick. Actually sick."

"I know," she said, way too pleased with herself. "I had it made. You're welcome."

I looked down at it for a second too long, turning my wrist a little, watching the little rose catch the light from the kitchen.

I didn't tell her. Didn't tell anybody.

Just added it to the pocket. Heaviest stone yet.

## XIV. Present — Roman's Room, Before School

*[I finally got up off the toilet and got moving for real, and everything felt loud in a way mornings don't usually feel loud. The water hitting the sink sounded almost musical, every drop distinct instead of just a blur of running water. I could smell the mint of the toothpaste before I even opened the cap, sharp and clean, cutting through everything else — the fabric softener smell still clinging to the towel, the faint trace of Mom's perfume from somewhere down the hall, layered under the coffee she'd already started.*

*I brushed my teeth slower than I needed to, just noticing it. The bristles against my gums, the exact texture of it, more detail than brushing my teeth had ever had before in my entire life.*

*I picked the card back up off the edge of the sink and read it over again, even though I basically had it memorized already.*

*"This key symbolizes that the power is in your hands. That only you hold the key that will open the realm that you've always wanted to enter. You are in control. It's your destiny. Believe in yourself. Trust your instincts. Good luck tomorrow, I'll be there for you if you need me. Happy Birthday Romy! Love you! — RoRo!"*

*I ran my thumb over the little rose again, over the petals, and thought about how wrong all of it already was, twelve hours after she wrote it, and how she had no way of knowing that yet. Neither did I, really. Not the size of it.*

*I'm in control, I thought. Sure.*

*I set the card back down on the sink, and back in my room, I got the clothes down off the hanger — the jeans, the Basquiat shirt, the Jordans still sitting in the box — and even the denim felt different against my hands, stiffer, more textured, every individual thread there for the feeling if I paid attention long enough. I didn't used to notice things at that level. I don't know when I started.*

*I locked the bracelet back around my wrist last, the key clicking into place, closing the circle.]*

Roman sat under the third table, back against the cinderblock wall, thumb moving over the little silver rose on his wrist without him really deciding to do it — just something for his hand to hold onto that wasn't his own knee. The door at the end of the hall had gone quiet for a second, and then it banged again, harder this time, the whole frame of it shuddering.

Somebody under the next table whimpered. Roman didn't look up. He just kept running his thumb over the rose, over and over, treating it as a rosary instead of jewelry.

*[I blinked the picture away — didn't know why my brain kept doing that, offering up a table and a door and a shooter that hadn't happened yet, some sick preview nobody had asked for — and kept getting ready, because there wasn't anything else to do with the time except keep moving forward.]*

## XV. Present — Roman's Room, Morning of Day One

Fully dressed now, just a couple minutes left before Mom really would start yelling, I noticed something move near the ceiling — a spider, small, crawling along the crack where the wall met the paint, minding its own business.

I stared at it without meaning to stare, my eyes just landing there and refusing to move on. And the zoom happened again, easy this time, almost gentle compared to the shelf — the whole ceiling narrowing, the ordinary blur of the room falling away, until it was just the spider, huge and close, every hair on its legs distinct, two tiny black eyes catching light I didn't even know was in the room.

I didn't gasp this time. I didn't flinch.

I just watched it, and for a second — just a second — I was steering it, not just riding it out. Maybe if I stopped fighting it, the drowning feeling would let up too.

That should've scared me more than it did.

Grabbed the new journal off my desk and shoved it into my backpack, in the front pocket, where I could feel the corner of it against my back the second I put the bag on.

"Let's go, kids, now now now!" Mom's voice came down the hall, half singing it, half meaning it.

Rosie stuck her head into my doorway before I even got my shoes fully on, already dressed, hair done, backpack over one shoulder. She looked at me for a second — really looked, her usual full-attention once-over — and whatever she saw must've read as nerves, because she softened.

"Let's do this," she said, and held her fist out.

I bumped it. Didn't correct her about what she thought she was seeing.

---

I sat in the back, forehead almost against the window, watching Bergenline go by in that half-blur cars make when you're not really looking at anything specific. Mom drove with one hand on the wheel and one eye on the rearview, checking me every few seconds as if a mirror could actually tell her anything about what was going on in my head.

"You're quiet back there," she said.

"I'm good."

"Freshman year," Rosie said from the front seat, twisting around to look at me properly. "I remember my first day. I threw up before we even left the house, no lie. Actual vomit."

"That's disgusting."

"That's real. And then by lunch I already had, like, three friends. It's not that deep, Romy. Everybody's new. Everybody's scared. Nobody's watching you as hard as you think they are."

"She's right," Mom said. "First day's the worst part. After that it's just Tuesday."

"Honestly," I said, going for light, going for a laugh, "I don't even wanna go. Can we just turn around."

Rosie snorted, already turning back to face front. "Oh my God, everybody says that. You'll be fine, drama king."

Mom didn't laugh. I caught her eyes flick to the rearview again, longer this time, something tightening around them that hadn't been there a second ago. "Why wouldn't you want to go? Is it your head? You feeling okay?"

"Ma, I'm joking."

"I'm just asking."

"I'm fine. I was kidding." I wasn't, not really, but but I couldn't explain why, so the lie came out easier than it should have.

She let it go, on the surface. Said "okay" and turned back toward the road as if that settled it. But I could hear her breathing change — not dramatic, nothing anybody without what I had going on could've picked up on, just a fraction shorter than it'd been a second ago, held a half-beat longer before letting go, the specific rhythm of somebody trying to seem calm instead of actually being calm. I don't think she even knew she was doing it. That's not a sound you're supposed to be able to hear at all. But I did, clear as anything — one more thing I now knew about somebody that they hadn't actually told me.

I nodded and let them think that's what it was. Let them build me a whole staircase out of first-day-nerves logic when the actual thing sitting in my chest didn't have stairs, didn't have a bottom I could see.

We pulled up outside North Bergen High School, the sidewalk already thick with kids in first-day fits, backpacks that still had that unscuffed newness to them. Rosie leaned over and kissed Mom's cheek. "Love you. Thanks for the ride."

"Love you too. Have a good first day, seniors get treated different, you know that."

I got out, walked around to the driver's side for my kiss. Mom held my face in both hands for a second longer than she needed to.

"You're gonna be fine," she said. "You hear me? Fine."

I nodded, because I didn't trust my voice not to give something away.

Rosie caught up to me before I made it more than a few steps, grabbing my arm to slow me down. "Hey." She waited until I actually looked at her. "If you need me today — whatever, any reason, doesn't have to be a big deal — you text me or you call me. Okay?" My phone buzzed a second later; she was already sending her schedule over. "That's my whole day, period by period. If you got a free period or a study hall or whatever, come find me. Or just text and I'll say I gotta use the bathroom and I'll come to you. Whatever it takes. I don't care." She bumped my shoulder, softer than usual. "Just know I'm here. Okay? Love ya. See you later."

She spotted somebody up ahead — a couple girls from her group chat, already waving her over — and jogged off before I could really answer, backpack bouncing, calling "love ya, bye!" back over her shoulder without even really looking at me anymore.

I walked toward the entrance. When I glanced back once, Mom was still parked there, not pulling off yet, already pulling her phone back out, ready to call somebody, watching me go, bottom lip caught between her teeth, eyebrows pulled together, worried about something she couldn't name.

## XVI. Return to Cafeteria — Present, Continuous

"Mom, I gotta go. I'll call you back."

"No — no, don't hang up, Roman, please, just stay on the phone with me, I don't care if you don't say anything, just stay on—"

He hung up anyway. He had to. A phone pressed to his ear was going to be the wrong kind of loud in about ten seconds, and whatever came next, his mother's voice wasn't going to be able to follow him into it.

He shoved the phone into his pocket and looked around at the faces under the table with him. Andrew was two feet away, knees pulled to his chest, jaw clenched so tight it looked painful, eyes wet but not falling yet. A couple of the girls from B lunch were pressed together near the far table leg, one of them silently mouthing something — a prayer, or her mother's name, or both. Somebody's phone lit up with a text and got shoved face-down into a lap so fast it was almost violent.

Fifth period lunch — Andrew's, same as Roman's, the one overlap they'd found comparing schedules over text a few days before school even started. Jude had a different lunch entirely; the two of them only had eighth period gym together. One more thing Roman would end up grateful for later, grateful and sick about it in the same breath, once he understood what the alternative could have been.

Roman's backpack was still wedged against his knee. He could feel the corner of the journal through the canvas, a small hard shape that meant nothing right now and somehow still mattered that it was there.

The door banged again — the third time now, each one closer together than the last, whoever was on the other side clearly done testing and moving on to deciding.

Then came the shots. Two of them, flat and close, aimed low, and the sound of the lock giving way landed almost anticlimactic after all that buildup — a small metallic clatter, nothing dramatic, nothing you'd get in a movie.

The door swung open.

For one full second, nobody under the table moved. The whole cafeteria seemed to hold something collectively, all five tables of it, a held breath with fifty different people's lungs behind it.

Then a shape stepped through the doorway, backlit by the hallway fluorescents, and the held breath broke apart into screaming.

---

He was closer to the tables on the right, the ones nearest the door he'd just come through, and that's where he went first — not aiming exactly, just turning and firing into anything that moved. A boy near the second table caught it in the shoulder and went down hard, dragging a chair with him. Screaming turned into a different kind of screaming, higher, more animal.

Under the third table, Roman's eyes cut sideways toward the second exit — the one along the far wall, past the row of vending machines, only ten or fifteen feet of open cafeteria floor between here and there. Close. Doable, if they moved fast enough. He looked at Andrew and jerked his head toward it, once, sharp.

Andrew shook his head. Fast. Terrified. *No.*

The shooter had moved further right, chasing down kids who'd broken from under their tables and were sprinting for the windows, the back exit, the kitchen doors flung wide with cooks waving people through, one of them still gripping a serving spoon, having apparently forgotten it was even in his hand. For a few seconds his back was fully turned, gun tracking a group of fleeing juniors near the serving line.

Roman moved.

He didn't decide to, not really — his body just went, low and fast, and Andrew, given no choice once Roman was already three feet clear of the table, scrambled up after him along with two girls who'd been crouched beside them. They ran in a loose line toward the second door, backpacks slapping against spines, somebody's shoe coming half off and getting left behind.

They were maybe eight feet from the door when the shot came.

It didn't sound aimed. It sounded almost incidental, one more round fired into a room already full of bodies in motion — and Andrew made a sound Roman would spend the rest of his life trying not to remember exactly, a wet, cut-off groan, and then he was falling, and there was nothing gradual about it, no slow-motion movie moment, just Andrew's whole weight coming down on top of Roman before either of them had finished the thought that this was happening.

They went down together. Roman's back hit the floor and Andrew landed half across his chest, half against his shoulder, close enough that Roman was looking directly into his face, close enough to count individual eyelashes, close enough to watch something behind Andrew's eyes go from present to absent in the space of maybe four seconds.

Blood came from the back of his head first, spreading fast against the tile. Then from his nose. Then his mouth, a thin dark line of it at the corner, and his lips were moving, or trying to, no sound coming out anymore, just breath with no words left attached to it.

One of the girls behind them had gone down too, a few feet short of the door, bleeding from her back, not moving.

Roman didn't scream. He didn't cry. He found out later that his body had apparently decided screaming was a waste of whatever time was left, and instead everything in him just went very, very still — terror first, then grief, thick and total, and then, underneath both of those, something else arriving to replace them, cold water displacing warm in a bathtub, gradual and then suddenly complete.

He felt himself go empty.

And then he felt himself fill back up with something that wasn't fear anymore.

He turned his head. The shooter was standing near the water vending machine, thirty feet off now, gun still raised, still tracking other bodies, still working through the room with the flat efficiency of a man running a checklist.

Time did the thing again — or maybe it was him doing it to time, he still couldn't tell which — except this time it didn't just slow, it nearly stopped. A fly, of all things, drifted through the space between them at what should have been ordinary fly-speed, and instead Roman watched each individual wingbeat separately, watched the small iridescent shudder of it hang suspended in the air, frozen there as if set in glass.

Roman's vision did the thing it had done in the bodega, the thing it had done on his ceiling — but bigger now, total, no resistance left in him to fight it with. The whole cafeteria narrowed to a single bright line, and at the center of it: the shooter, turning, slow as a door swinging shut in a dream, some late animal instinct finally catching up to what his body should have felt minutes ago. Roman watched the turn complete itself in pieces — the shoulder first, then the hips, then the head coming around last, dragged by all the rest of it — and somewhere in the middle of that turn the shooter's eyes found him and widened, and the last thing that man would ever see clearly was the mouth of something eighteen inches long where a fourteen-year-old's face should have been.

Roman could see everything in those eyes. Pale blue, and underneath the fear now finally arriving too late, nothing. Empty — nothing to do with time slowing down. — that emptiness had probably always been there, Roman understood, distant and strange, Roman only let himself see it now that he wasn't afraid of the man anymore. A single bead of sweat, frozen mid-track, halfway down the side of his temple.

The gun started to come up. Started to turn back toward Roman, too late by any measure that mattered, an entire slow-motion lifetime too late.

Heat built at the base of Roman's skull — the same heat as the migraines, he thought, distantly, from somewhere still recognizably his own. But bigger. Volcanic. And this time, instead of dropping him to his knees, it filled him standing up.

He stopped fighting it.

There was no weighing anything this time. No two coins, no holding both sides until he found what was underneath. For the first time in his entire careful, thinking, turn-it-over-twice life, some other version of him reached for the wheel and didn't ask permission — and some small, still-human part of him, watching from somewhere very far away now, understood that whatever came next wasn't going to be him making a choice. It was going to be a decision already made, arriving through him instead of from him.

---

It started at his jaw.

Somewhere against his hip, distant and small and completely unable to compete with what was happening to the rest of him, his phone buzzed once — a sound from another room, barely registering. There was no part of him left available to check it. There was barely a him left available for anything.

A pulling sensation first, deep under the skin, something coiled tight inside him his whole life finally, finally given permission to unwind. His face went numb, then hot, then not his anymore — bone rearranging itself, a muffled underwater crack of knuckles, cartilage stretching into shapes a human skull was never built to hold. Skin darkened and hardened, losing color, losing softness, taking on a dull gunmetal sheen that caught the fluorescent light harder than flesh ever had.

He rose.

Whatever was left of the boy who'd been lying on that floor a half-second earlier folded itself into something else entirely — his body dropping low into a crouch, weight balanced forward onto the balls of his feet, shoulders squared, no fourteen-year-old left in the stance at all, only something built for exactly one purpose. Energy gathered visibly along his forearms, the air over them shimmering with the same faint distortion heat throws off asphalt, building, concentrating, refusing to be anywhere but where it was headed.

His face was gone. In its place, a barrel — massive, seamless, extending a full eighteen or twenty inches out from where his face used to be, fused seamlessly to what remained of his skull, waiting fourteen years for the rest of him to catch up. The metal of it had no name Roman knew — not gunmetal, not steel, nothing he'd ever seen in his life — a dull, deep gray with something almost oily shifting underneath the surface when the light hit it wrong, engineering out of a period nobody on Earth had gotten to yet. And still, somehow, he could see. Perfectly. The whole room in total clarity, the crosshair steady and unwavering, locked dead center on the shooter's flat blue eyes.

The shooter's gun was still rising, still chasing a target it was never going to reach in time — that same slow, doomed arc Roman had already watched begin an eternity ago, a half-second that had somehow lasted this long.

He didn't reach it.

The blast that came out of Roman was not loud so much as total. This wasn't a gunshot. This was the room itself breaking. For one full frame of time, still stretched impossibly thin, Roman watched the shooter's body fail to survive what was happening to it — the torso opening from the inside before the outside had caught up to the idea of it, ribs blown outward in fragments, a wet dark spray of viscera and blood misting the air ahead of the rest of him, brain and bone following a half-beat later in a spray that reached the ceiling tiles and stayed there. The man was two separate ideas of a body for a fraction of a second — the shape he'd been, and the ruin already outracing it — before both of them were swallowed into what happened to the wall.

The water vending machine went with him, and the cinderblock wall behind that — the wall didn't stop the blast so much as fail to, the force punching straight through into the kitchen beyond it, where two of the staff who'd been holding that back exit open, waving kids through only seconds before, never had time to move. All of it gone in the same instant, folding outward into dust and twisted metal and a hole where a wall used to be, the shooter's remains scattered across all of it so thoroughly that nothing recognizably human would be left for anyone to identify by sight.

Roman didn't know, in that half-second, what else the blast had taken with it. He couldn't have. He only knew the recoil, distant and strange, moving through a body that no longer entirely belonged to the boy who'd woken up that morning.

---

The barrel receded first, folding itself back into a jaw, a mouth, a face — his face, slack now, drained of whatever had filled it a moment before. His knees buckled almost immediately after, the crouch collapsing in on itself, and he went down hard onto the tile, onto his side, into a mess that was partly Andrew's blood and partly his own sweat and partly something that didn't have a name yet.

He didn't lose consciousness gracefully. It was a phone dying — one second running, the next simply not.

He lay there in his new jeans, soaked dark at the knee where Andrew had gone down against him, in the Basquiat shirt his mother had picked out with such care the day before, most of the front of it no longer any color she'd recognize, in the Jordans still box-fresh under all that red. The silver rose on his wrist caught light from somewhere in the wreckage, one clean bright thing in the middle of everything that wasn't. Around him, the cafeteria kept happening without him — sirens outside now, radios crackling, boots on tile, someone shouting for a medic near the second exit, someone else shouting a name that wasn't his.

His phone had slid halfway out of his pocket in the fall, screen still lit, still facing up. It buzzed once against the tile, then again — **ROSIE**, stacked twice over on the lock screen, each one arriving on top of the last, some frantic effort to make up for something.

He didn't answer. Couldn't. He was already gone somewhere the sound couldn't reach, his sister's name lighting up an empty little rectangle beside a boy who wasn't available for any of it anymore.

---

## XVII. Institutional Cutaway

The detective arrived forty minutes after the first units, once the building had been cleared and the wounded triaged, and he walked the scene with the same unhurried method he'd walked a hundred scenes before it — slow, hands clasped behind his back, letting his eyes do the work before his mouth did.

He stopped at the hole.

It wasn't a bullet hole. It wasn't a bomb crater, either, not exactly — the edges were too clean for that, the concrete not so much blown apart as *removed*, something having reached through the wall rather than into it. A vending machine sat crumpled fifteen feet from where it should have been, folded nearly in half. The kitchen beyond the wall was open to the cafeteria now, a room that wasn't supposed to have a door there suddenly had one, jagged and glowing faintly with settling dust.

He'd seen gang shootings. He'd seen a car bomb once, years back, upstate. Nothing in either had prepared him for this.

"Get me the cafeteria cameras," he said to the principal, who was standing a few feet off, his entire understanding of the world visibly rearranging itself in real time. "All of it. Every angle you got in this room."

It took twenty minutes to pull the footage onto a drive. The detective watched it once, the principal standing beside him, jaw tightening a little more with each new angle. He watched a boy transform. He watched a wall disappear. He watched the boy fold back into himself and drop to the floor, a puppet with its strings cut.

He took the drive. He deleted the original from the server himself, right there in front of the principal, offering no explanation and inviting no questions. The principal didn't ask for one. He just watched it happen with the particular stillness of someone who already knows better than to argue.

"This doesn't go anywhere," the detective said, still facing the now-empty screen. "Not to the superintendent. Not to your staff. Not to a single parent who calls asking what really happened in that cafeteria. As far as anyone outside this room knows, a kid got caught in a gas line rupture, or a structural collapse, or whatever story ends up in the paper by tomorrow. You didn't see what you just saw."

"And if someone asks me directly?"

"Then you don't know. That's not a suggestion." The detective finally looked at him. "Federal people are going to want this handled clean. Until they get here and tell you different, this stays between the two of us. You understand what I'm telling you?"

The principal nodded, slow, the color still not fully back in his face. He understood. Not the *how* of it — nobody in that room understood the how of it — but he understood the shape of the thing well enough to know that some doors, once you'd looked through them, closed themselves behind you whether you wanted them to or not.

Then the detective walked to his car, sat behind the wheel with the door still open, and took out his personal phone — not his department radio, not anything that would show up on a log anywhere official.

The call was picked up on the second ring.

"We've got a situation at a school shooting in North Bergen," the detective said, low, even. "Shooter's dead. But not from anything I can explain in a report. I've got footage. Kid's face turned into some kind of weapon. Took out a wall, some vending machines, killed two kitchen staff in the blast radius along with the shooter."

A pause on the other end. "Send me a name."

"Casador. Roman Casador. Freshman."

Nothing on the other end for a second. No recognition in it, no sharpening — just the particular silence of someone actually processing something instead of already knowing it.

"Copy the footage to a USB drive," the voice said finally. "Not a cloud upload, not an email, physical drive only, and you guard that thing with your life until it's collected. Nobody touches it. Nobody views it again. You understand?"

"Copy that."

"One more thing." A pause, the voice weighing how much to say. "Wherever the ambulances are taking the injured — Hackensack, I'd guess, that's the closer one — make sure this kid doesn't go there. Get him to Holy Name instead. Divert it if you have to. Tell them whatever you need to tell them."

The detective frowned at the windshield. "That's a fifteen-minute difference on a kid who might need a trauma team right now."

"He'll be fine at Holy Name. Make it happen. We'll handle it from there."

The line went dead before the detective could ask anything else.

He sat there another second with the engine still off, staring straight ahead at nothing in particular, running the math on how strange it was that whoever he'd just talked to seemed to know exactly which hospital not to use before anyone had even said the kid was hurt, and then he put the car in drive.

---

Three states away, in an office with no nameplate on the door, the agent set the phone down and sat there a second before crossing the hall. He was big — six-four, He was big — six-four, still carrying the swagger of a college linebacker a decade removed from actually playing, University of Wisconsin, a name he dropped more than he probably should in casual conversation. He liked this job, for the same reason some men like contact sports — for the hit, not just the win.

His supervisor looked up from her desk when he knocked. Carrie Ann Killiard — Killer Ann, to everyone who worked under her, though never within earshot — dressed head to toe in black, same as always, a thin leather wristband on her left wrist that she never took off, a birthday gift from her partner years back. She had the build of someone who didn't lose arguments, and she generally didn't.

"We have something," he said. "North Bergen. School shooting, shooter's dead, but a kid did it. Not with a weapon — with his own body. Best I can describe it, some kind of gun came out of his head. Like his whole face turned into the barrel. I know how that sounds. You're gonna want to see the footage yourself."

She didn't ask him to describe it further. She just asked, "Where's the kid now?"

"Getting rerouted to Holy Name instead of Hackensack, per your standing directive on anything like this."

Something crossed her face — not recognition, not a name she already knew, but something colder: the specific attention of someone who has spent years waiting for exactly this kind of call and had started to wonder if it would ever actually come.

"Get the drive picked up tonight," she said. "And put a hold on that kid's chart the second he's admitted. Nobody touches him without us in the room."

"And if the hospital asks why?"

"They won't. Not once they see whose name is attached to the request." She was already reaching for her phone. "Let's find out what we've got."
