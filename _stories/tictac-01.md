The doorbell rang twice before Simone got to it, which never happened. Usually she was faster than that.

She unhooked the chain, cracked the door to the width of her own shoulder, and took the bag from the delivery man without really looking at his face. He said something about the tip already being on the app. She didn't manage a thank you — didn't trust her voice not to give her away.

She didn't close the door all the way. She stood there instead, the bag hooked over one wrist, the smell of sesame oil and scallions rising up warm against her chin, and fixed her eyes on the street, gripped by the sudden, senseless fear that if she looked away, her mother wouldn't come back at all.

There was a mark on the collar of her white blouse, just under her jaw, dark and irregular. She'd caught herself wiping at it once with a wet thumb, then stopped — something in her already certain that no amount of spit was going to lift it. She hadn't looked at it since it happened. She wasn't going to start now.

Behind her, the house was too quiet for a house with three kids in it. Upstairs, Shayna and Darrell were still shut in their room, waiting for their sister to come back.

Headlights swung into the driveway. Her mother's car, the one with the dent over the back wheel that Daryl kept saying he'd get fixed and never did. Simone didn't move. She stayed exactly where she was, in the doorway, food in hand. She didn't dare move. Some fragile, childish logic told her that if she just held still, tonight hadn't technically started yet.

Jessica cut the engine, gathered her bag off the passenger seat, and looked up toward the house in her usual ritual scan — checking the porch light, checking for her kids — and she found Simone standing there in the open door and smiled. A real one. Tired, but real. The smile of a woman coming home to something ordinary.

Simone looked back at her mother's face doing that, and felt her own face do nothing at all.

A smile arriving gift-wrapped, and her face simply forgetting how to open for it. Right now, in this doorway, with the food going lukewarm against her hip and her mother already halfway up the walk saying something cheerful about traffic on the Parkway, all Simone could do was hold the door open and wait for her to get close enough to see.

*Three weeks earlier.*

---

Daryl was good at Legos, which was maybe the most unfair thing about him.

He was down on the carpet with Darrell, both of them cross-legged in front of a half-built spaceship, and he wasn't doing that thing some dads did where they took over and built it themselves while the kid watched. He waited. He let Darrell jam a piece in wrong three times before he said anything, and when he finally reached over it was just to turn the piece around in Darrell's own small hand, not take it from him — *try it this way, bud* — patience that visibly cost him something to give, the currency of a man who needed to be needed.

Darrell was radiant with it, narrating the spaceship's entire fictional crew roster unprompted, an audience finally worth performing for.

Simone sat on the arm of the couch with a book open on her knee, not reading it, watching the back of Daryl's head instead. She did this now. She hadn't decided to; it had just started happening sometime over the summer, a low hum of attention that never fully switched off no matter what room he was in.

*He's good with Darrell,* she thought, and hated how much that single fact tipped the whole scale.

"Shayna, baby, come sit, you're missing the whole mission."

Shayna looked up from her own corner of the carpet, where she'd been half-heartedly coloring a horse purple. She didn't move right away — Simone noticed that, filed it, the half-second where her sister's eyes went somewhere private before her body followed.

"I'm coloring."

"Bring the horse. Come here."

Shayna came. She always came, in the end — not because she wanted to, but because not-coming announced something on its own, and she was eight, without the tools yet to make that announcement on purpose.

She folded herself into the gap between his crossed legs and his chest, knees together, the purple crayon still clenched in one fist, a small anchor. Daryl kept talking to Darrell about the spaceship without missing a beat, one arm settling around Shayna loosely, and for a few seconds the tableau held — ordinary, unremarkable, exactly engineered to read that way.

Then Shayna went still. Not the stillness of rest — a held-breath stillness, her shoulders climbing half an inch toward her ears, the crayon frozen in her hand.

Simone's own book had stopped existing in her lap entirely.

She counted without meaning to — a habit she didn't have a name for yet, wouldn't name for weeks, until a summer storm finally handed her the words: the gap between flash and sound, the distance a body measures by how long it has to wait for the second half of a thing. One second. Two. On three, Shayna's whole small body decided something at once — a squirm, sudden and total, more escape than movement — and she twisted sideways out of the gap and onto the carpet beside him, upright in a single motion, already reaching back down for the dropped crayon as though nothing in the last ten seconds had happened.

"I gotta finish the horse," she said, to no one, to the carpet, and went back to her corner.

Daryl didn't look over at Simone. He didn't need to. He picked the spaceship back up mid-sentence with Darrell, and Shayna's flight off his lap became weather — something that had passed through the room and left no mark on it.

Simone sat on the arm of the couch, her heart going hard in a chest that hadn't moved at all, and felt something arrive right behind the fear, quieter but just as fast: *maybe you're the one making this into something.* He hadn't grabbed her. He hadn't said anything. Eight-year-olds wriggled off of everything — chairs, car seats, church pews — restless by nature, unremarkable by design. Maybe this was just what Simone did now: took ordinary, boring moments and built a monster out of them because some part of her needed somewhere to put a feeling she didn't have a name for.

She almost believed it. She wanted to, with the same appetite a stomachache has for being nothing more than something she ate.

But she looked at her sister's shoulders, still faintly climbed toward her ears even now, safe, ten feet away, purple crayon dragging too fast and too hard against the horse's mane — and some steadier, older part of Simone, a part uninterested in what she wanted to believe, filed it anyway, alongside all the others.

Three. That was how long it had taken this time.

She went back to her book and didn't read a single word of it.

I used to pray every night, flat on my back in the dark, eyes open — not Mom's kneeling method — running through a checklist: thank you for today, watch over Darrell and Shayna, watch over Mom, help me get better at math.

Somewhere in the last few weeks the list had grown a new line, and I hated that it had, and I asked it anyway, every night, the same words in the same order, an incantation I trusted more than I trusted myself.

*Please make him stop.*

I never said what "it" was. I didn't need to. That was the whole point of Him — omniscient, already watching — so spelling it out seemed less a matter of information and more an act of forcing Him to watch it again on purpose, and I didn't want to do that to either of us. I kept it small and vague, a child's version of asking for good weather. Please make him stop. Please let me be wrong about what I think I saw. Please let Shayna be okay. Please, please, please.

He didn't stop. Obviously he didn't stop — the prayer changed nothing, and the nights kept happening exactly the way they always had, two of them some weeks, sometimes three — but I prayed regardless, the nights it happened and the nights it didn't, sending the same message twice just in case the first one hadn't landed. I lay in the dark afterward running my little list and getting nothing back but the house settling around me.

The night I finally said it — out loud, in a whisper too flat to pass for prayer anymore, a message sent to a number that had stopped replying years ago — I said, *Dear God, I guess you're not getting my texts.* And then, because that almost made me laugh — the single worst possible thing to feel in that moment — I added: *Well, then. I'm done sending them.*

I didn't pray again after that. Not out of anger. It was closer to what happens when you finally stop texting somebody back: not fury, just an exhausted end to hoping. I didn't block Him, nothing so dramatic. I just stopped opening the thread, left it sitting there unread at the bottom of everything else, unpinned, however many nights it had been since I last checked it.

---

Two days later I got my period. My body picked the middle of silent reading to announce itself, apparently having waited for the single worst moment on the calendar — the whole class quiet enough to hear a page turn. I sat there a second not understanding what was wrong, then understanding all at once, and asked Mrs. Delgado, in the smallest voice I owned, if I could go to the nurse. The nurse was overly, professionally cheerful — here's a pad, here's a spare pair of shorts, you're not the first girl this has happened to today, sweetheart, believe me. I believed her. I still wanted to disappear into the tile floor.

I called Mom from the nurse's office. She picked up on the first ring, and something in her voice — softer, faster, thrilled underneath the sympathy — told me this was a day she'd been anticipating for years, circled somewhere on a private calendar. She picked me up early, bought me ice cream on the way home, an oddly celebratory reward for something that mostly just hurt, and told me a story about the day it had happened to her that I only half absorbed, my stomach doing something I didn't have words for yet.

I didn't think anything else about it. Why would I. Girls get their period. It's biology, not an omen.

---

The day after, Darrell went down the back steps too fast — sneakers, wet leaves, his feet going out from under him — and split his bottom lip open on the concrete edge. Shayna's scream arrived first, then Darrell's, stacked directly on top of each other, a two-part alarm that should have scared me more than it did.

It didn't. Something in me went quiet and exact instead, a room clearing itself before anyone starts giving directions.

"Shayna, ice — freezer, the tray in the door." I had Darrell's face turned up before I'd finished the sentence, checking the cut with two fingers, gentle, clinical, careful in the exact manner I'd watched the school nurse be careful, twice now. Shallow. Messy — lips always bled worse than they actually were. "You're okay. Look at me. You're okay. It's just a lip."

Shayna came back with the ice wrapped, badly, in a dish towel, breathing hard, the kitchen apparently farther away than it actually was. Darrell was already calming, hiccuping instead of wailing, because I hadn't given him a single reason not to.

We had him cleaned up and parked in front of the TV, ice pressed to his mouth, before Mom's car turned onto the block. I never thought about it as anything at all. Somebody has to be the one who doesn't panic. Somewhere in the last few months, quietly, without my noticing exactly when, that had become me.

---

It was maybe four days after that when I noticed the doorknob.

My bedroom door sticks — always has, you have to shoulder it on the way out or the latch catches wrong. I did it without thinking, same as every morning, and the whole knob came off in my hand — clean, complete, coming away with no resistance at all — as if it had never been screwed in to begin with. I stood there at seven in the morning holding a doorknob, feeling idiotic, certain I'd broken something, already rehearsing an apology to Mom about the door.

That same week at school, Marcus Aiello bet me I couldn't open his water bottle, the extra-tight kind his dad brings home from work. I did it one-handed without really trying, and something crossed his face — a small recalculation, the look of a boy revising what he thought he knew. I laughed it off, blamed his grip. But I remember flexing my hand under the desk afterward, privately, checking it for something I couldn't have named if you'd asked me.

The other thing was worse, because it lived in my head instead of my hands.

I'd be sitting in class and suddenly I'd have finished the worksheet three questions ahead of where my pencil actually was, my brain lapping my hand, indifferent to whatever the rest of me was doing. Ms. Okafor called on me in Social Studies and I answered so fast, kept going so fast, that she held up a hand and said, "Simone. Breathe. This isn't a race." The class laughed a little — not unkind, just surprised — and I sat back down with my thoughts still running somewhere out ahead of me, waiting for the rest of me to arrive.

I didn't tell anybody about any of it. Not Mom, not my friends, not even really myself, if that makes sense — I noticed it and then I put it in a drawer in my head next to the other thing I wasn't looking at either, the one with Daryl's name on it, and I told myself both drawers could stay shut a while longer. I had gotten pretty good, by then, at keeping drawers shut.

I didn't pray about the doorknob, or the water bottle, or my brain running laps without me. I'd already sent my last text on that account.

I just kept noticing. Filing. Waiting to see what came next.

---

Shayna had been beating me for twenty minutes and would not shut up about it.

"You can't get this one," she sang, dragging the threat out long enough to enjoy it, and then she kicked it — really kicked it, her whole little body behind the swing — and the ball went up instead of across, way up, arcing toward the fence on some private errand of its own.

I didn't think. I just went.

One second my feet were in the grass, and the next they weren't — and instead of coming back down, the ordinary business of jumping, I stayed. Four feet up, maybe five, arms still reaching, the air itself doing the catching for once instead of the ground doing the falling. I could see over the fence. I could see Darrell's dump truck army lined up by the tomato plants. I could see my own shadow on the grass below, small and unfamiliar, a stranger's shadow borrowing my shape.

It probably lasted two seconds. It felt endless — longer than that, even: two seconds that had somehow learned to stretch.

Then gravity remembered I existed. I dropped, caught the ball against my chest on the way down, and hit the ground rolling, smooth enough to pass for rehearsed.

"OH MY GOD you got so high!" Shayna was already doubled over laughing, hands on her knees. "You looked like a cartoon! Do it again!"

"Skills," I said, my voice coming out normal — a small magic trick in itself, since my heart was going so hard I could feel it in my teeth.

Darrell had stopped mid-truck-crash to stare at me, his mouth already open on a question he didn't have the words for yet.

"You flied," he said finally.

"I jumped, dummy. Everybody jumps." I threw the ball at his head, gentle, just to make him laugh instead of think. It worked. He forgot about it in four seconds flat — five-year-olds forget everything except what's happening right now.

I didn't forget. I stood there holding a soccer ball, my smile for my little sister fully intact on the surface, and underneath it I was doing math I didn't have the numbers for yet.

---

Daryl picks us up from school most days — me and Shayna from the grammar school first, then around the corner for Darrell at the kindergarten — and by the time we pull into the driveway, Darrell's usually asleep or halfway there, cranky and boneless in his car seat, extraction its own small production. That Monday I had to pee so badly I spent the whole ride home with my knees pressed together, pretending to sit normally, glaring at every red light, each one a small, deliberate insult.

The second the car stopped I had my seatbelt off and the door open, moving before I'd fully decided to move.

"Simone, wait, help me with—" Shayna, from somewhere behind me, still wrestling her backpack out from under her feet.

I didn't wait. I threw out a "sorry, gotta pee," already three steps gone, Daryl's voice trailing behind me as he leaned into the back seat, working Darrell's buckle, half of him still folded into the car.

I don't really remember running across the driveway. That's the strange part, reconstructing it afterward — there's the car door, and then there's the front door, and between them, nothing. No memory of my feet on the pavement or my hand finding the doorknob. Just a blank where all of that should be.

What I do remember is standing at the top of the stairs, hand already on the bathroom door, not even breathing hard, thinking the one dumb, useful thought a body has room for when it's entirely focused on not peeing itself: *huh, that was fast.*

I didn't return to it until I was sitting there, door locked, the adrenaline of almost-not-making-it draining out of me, and in that quiet the *huh, that was fast* circled back around and turned into something else. I've run up those stairs a thousand times. I know exactly what that climb costs: the thump of my own feet, my thighs beginning to burn around step ten, the small out-of-breath huff at the top. None of it had happened. A whole reel had gone missing — the middle cut clean out, the beginning spliced straight to the end.

From outside, faintly, muffled through the bathroom door and the window and however many walls stood between us, I could hear Shayna's voice, confused, rising into a question at the end. "Simone? Simone, where'd you—" and then something I couldn't make out, and then, quieter, mostly to herself, "she was just right there."

I sat there a second longer than I needed to, washing my hands slower than I needed to, studying myself in the mirror, searching my own face for an answer that might be written there, if I looked hard enough.

It wasn't. Just my own face, tired around the eyes same as it had been lately, nothing else new in it.

I filed it with the doorknob and the water bottle and the racing thoughts in class, in the same drawer, and went downstairs to help with Darrell, already performing normal — getting good at that part too, now. I'd had plenty of practice.

---

At four-fifteen, Daryl was playing house.

He'd have Jessica know exactly how much he'd done today — plates fanned across the coffee table, arranged with a magazine's precision, napkins folded into triangles Simone had never once seen him bother with, forks laid at careful angles that were, somehow, still art. Water bottles sweating in a neat row, sealed and waiting. A performance staged for an audience of one who wasn't home yet, rehearsed for a woman who'd walk in an hour from now and think, *look how he takes care of us.*

Simone sat cross-legged on the far end of the couch with a math worksheet open on her knees, not doing it, watching the room instead. Darrell was on the floor army-crawling a dump truck through invisible terrain, narrating the mission to himself in a low, serious voice. The TV ran a cartoon nobody was really watching. Everything about the room insisted it was just a Monday.

"Shayna, baby, come here."

Shayna didn't move right away. She'd gotten slower about it lately, a half-second of hesitation Simone had started counting — the same tally she kept between lightning and thunder, measuring how close the storm actually was. But she went — she always went, because going quickly was easier than what happened when you didn't — and climbed up onto his lap, and sat there with her spine too straight and her hands gripped tight around her own knees, eyes locked on the television, willing the screen to pull her out of the room by sheer force of staring.

Simone's pencil stopped moving.

She reached for the same reassurance she reached for every time: that watching was only watching because someone had to, that it counted as doing something, even when it never turned into anything else. Shayna shifted. Went still. Shifted again, smaller this time, a private, wordless struggle with nothing playful in it.

Something in Simone's chest cracked through months of practiced blankness all at once — pressure that had been building against a single seam, finding it.

Daryl looked over and caught it.

He didn't flinch. He didn't reach for the embarrassment of a man caught at something shameful. He held Simone's eyes for one long, deliberate second, and then he winked at her — slow, almost affectionate, a wink that said *I see you seeing this, and it changes nothing, and there is not one thing you can do about it.* He turned his attention back to the television. Matter closed.

Something in Simone's head went white and quiet, the stillness right before a wave breaks.

She wasn't looking at Daryl. She was looking at the fork.

It sat on the coffee table exactly where he'd placed it, silver, ordinary, angled just so beside a folded napkin, and in the half-second before anything happened, Simone felt the weight of it, the cool flat plane of metal, a wrongness in her own chest reaching for a wrongness outside herself.

She didn't decide. There was no moment of aiming, no act of will she could have named afterward. It was closer to a held breath finally letting go.

The fork left the table.

It crossed the space between the coffee table and Daryl's face faster than the eye could properly follow, faster than sound, and the last thing that registered on his face before it hit was not fear — there wasn't time for fear — just the beginning of confusion, one eyebrow starting to lift, the wink not yet fully faded from the corner of his mouth.

It went through his left eye and didn't stop.

The sound he made lasted less than two seconds — something between a gasp and a question — and then his whole body went slack all at once, total stillness, the stillness of something that had been holding itself up and had simply stopped. Blood came in a fine, violent mist — a wound spraying too fast for blood to fall the ordinary way, and it reached farther than blood should have been able to reach — across Shayna's collar, dotting her cheek, and further still, flecking pale red across the white of Simone's blouse — a paintbrush shaken loose, dots without a pattern.

For one full second, nobody in the room made a sound at all.

Then Shayna started to scream.

---

Shayna's scream went on longer than seemed possible for something with lungs that small, a single unbroken note that filled the living room and drowned out thought, the television still playing to nobody, and Darrell's confused, delayed wail starting up a beat behind hers, an echo running late.

Simone was already moving — her legs deciding before the rest of her caught up.

"Hey. Hey, look at me." She had Shayna by both shoulders, turned bodily away from the couch, from what was on the couch, blocking as much of it as her own small frame could block. Shayna's scream cracked and dropped into ragged, hitching breaths, her whole body shaking under Simone's hands. "Look at me. Not there. Here."

Darrell had stopped crying with the abrupt, total halt native to toddlers, staring instead with the flat, uncomprehending focus of someone watching something too large to be afraid of yet.

"We're going upstairs," Simone said. Her own voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect, steadier than anything inside her felt. "Right now. Both of you."

She got them up the stairs with a hand on each of them, Shayna stumbling, Darrell silent and heavy-limbed, and into the bathroom, and ran the water warm over a washcloth with hands that she watched shake without quite believing they were hers. She wiped the red flecks from Shayna's collar and the side of her face with the ordinary, businesslike care of cleaning a scraped knee, not looking at what the cloth turned into.

"It was an accident," Simone said. "Something weird happened. That's all you know, okay? Something weird happened and it was an accident."

Shayna's eyes were huge, swimming, fixed on Simone's face — the only fixed point left in the room. "Is he—"

"Don't." Simone said it fast, before the rest of the sentence could exist out loud. "Shayna. Look at me. You don't tell Mom anything else. Just — an accident. Something weird. Can you do that?"

A long beat. Then a small, wrecked nod.

"Darrell, baby, you too, okay? Something weird happened."

Darrell didn't answer. Simone wasn't sure he'd understood any of it, and some smaller, older part of her was grateful for that, one more thing added to the drawer she was already learning not to open.

"Wait here," she told them both. "Both of you. Right here. I'll be right back."

She made herself walk back down the stairs at a normal pace, which took more out of her than the running upstairs had.

The living room looked wrong for reasons that had nothing to do with what had actually changed in it — the same coffee table, the same too-careful napkins, the television still going, cheerful and oblivious, some sitcom laugh track rolling out into a room that had no use for it anymore. Daryl was on his back where he'd fallen, one leg still bent up under the coffee table, staged entirely for how it needed to look rather than how he'd actually gone down.

Simone made herself look at the fork.

She crouched at his shoulder, braced for the effort it should have taken to shift a grown man's dead weight, and pushed — and he came up off the floor half a foot before her mind had even finished bracing for it, arms doing something her body hadn't asked permission for. She set him back down, startled, and tried it again on purpose this time, flat palms against his shoulder and ribs, pushing steady. He turned with the ease of a sack of laundry, easier even than that, and for one strange, suspended second Simone stood outside her own effort entirely, watching her own arms do this and marveling at them — a stranger's arms, borrowed briefly, performing someone else's trick.

She used it. She worked him fully onto his side and then his front, arranging the fall a body would make tripping forward over something left in its path — one arm bent wrong and pinned beneath him, legs still angled toward the wall where Darrell's truck sat, his head turned so the fork jutting from the ruin of his eye socket faced the corner of the coffee table close enough that a stumble could plausibly have driven his own face down onto it. She nudged the table half an inch, until its corner lined up with the angle his skull would have struck going down. She looked at the toy truck by the wall and, after a second's hesitation, dragged it three feet closer to where his feet would have caught it.

Somewhere in the house, a phone started buzzing.

It took her a second to place it — Daryl's, on the counter where he'd left it charging, screen lighting the kitchen wall in intervals. She could see the name from across the room without meaning to: **Jess.** It rang out its full cycle and went quiet, then, a few seconds later, started again.

Simone didn't move toward it. There was nothing she could say into that phone that would make any of this smaller, and some instinct told her that the version of this her mother needed to hear couldn't come from a stranger's voice picking up a dead man's cell.

She was still crouched by the coffee table, checking the angle of his arm against the angle a fall would actually produce, when the doorbell rang.

She froze. Then remembered — the food. He'd ordered Chinese, forty minutes ago, in a kitchen that had smelled of performance, a man staging a version of himself for an audience that hadn't gotten home yet.

Simone stood, wiped her palms down the front of her jeans, and walked to the door on legs that felt borrowed, not entirely her own. She left the chain latch on out of some flat, functional habit — her mother's rule, always check before you open all the way — and cracked the door against it.

A delivery driver stood on the step, phone in one hand, a paper bag already going translucent with grease in the other, checking a name against an address with the flat indifference of any delivery on any street.

"Floyd?"

Simone took the bag through the gap. Didn't manage a thank you. Closed the door most of the way, then, after a second, unhooked the chain — no reason for it now, nothing left inside worth locking anyone out from — and let it swing loose.

That was when she saw her mother's car turning into the driveway.

---

Simone was still standing in the doorway when her mother's car turned into the driveway, the bag of Chinese food going cold and greasy against her forearm, the chain latch swinging loose where she'd unhooked it a minute ago. She watched Jessica cut the engine, gather her purse and her cardigan off the passenger seat, and step out into a gray-gold November four-thirty, light with the quality of a memory forming in real time.

Jessica looked up at the house and smiled, the easy, reflexive smile of a woman coming home to her kids, lifting a hand in a little wave she didn't even seem to notice herself making.

Simone didn't smile back. She tried — felt her face attempt it, felt it fail somewhere before it reached her eyes — and then gave up trying altogether, because there was nothing in her left to spend on it.

"Hey, baby." Jessica came up the walk still talking, spilling the day out of herself before she was even through the door, same as always — something about the traffic on the Parkway, something about how she owed all three of them for putting up with her tonight. She reached out and brushed a thumb along Simone's jaw in passing, the automatic, unthinking touch of a mother checking that her child is still exactly where she left her, and stepped over the threshold.

Her eyes went where ten years of motherhood had trained them to go first. Not the living room. The stairs.

Darrell and Shayna were already halfway down, small hands laced together in the parking-lot grip she'd drilled into them, and for one full second everything in Jessica's chest loosened at the sight of them, safe, upright, hers.

Then she saw the blouse.

Shayna's shirt had gone from white to a washed-out pink, the color of something that had been red once and given up trying to stay that way. Jessica's mouth was already halfway into a smile when she said it, the joke arriving before the fear did — a mother's reflex, buying one more second of not knowing what she was looking at.

"So — were y'all painting?" What came out of her was a short, airless sound, looking for somewhere to land.

Simone said nothing. She stood very still by the door, food bag still hanging forgotten from her fingers, and stillness, on her, had never once meant calm.

It was Shayna who moved — just her eyes, just a fraction, from her mother's face out through the open living room doorway to the place on the floor she'd been made to promise not to look at again. Jessica followed the angle of that glance without meaning to, a body chasing a sound before the mind has caught up to why.

"Daryl?" She said it toward the living room, light at first, a question tossed down a hallway. Then again, quieter, with an edge under it now that hadn't been there a second ago. "Daryl, baby, you home?"

Simone put her hand flat against her mother's stomach — the flat-palmed instinct of pulling someone back from a curb.

"Mom." Her voice came out smaller than she meant it to. "Something happened. Something bad."

Jessica looked down at her daughter's hand as though it belonged to a stranger, and then she was moving, Simone's arm sliding uselessly off her as she crossed into the living room in three long strides, already saying his name a third time, already reaching for a light switch though the room was lit —

The sound she made didn't have a shape a person could write down. It started as a scream and then seemed to run out of air halfway through and kept going anyway, on nothing, on will alone.

Behind her, the stairs erupted — Darrell first, then Shayna, crying the breathless cry of children who don't understand what's wrong but understand completely that something is. Simone stood in the doorway with her hands open at her sides, tears cutting clean lines through the dust and blood dried at her hairline, and she started talking because the silence had become unbearable.

"It was an accident, Mom. I didn't mean to—"

She stopped. There was no version of the next word that wouldn't be a lie, and some part of her, even now, even here, couldn't make herself say one to her mother's face.

Jessica turned from the body slowly, turning itself a physical cost, and looked at her daughter standing in the doorway soaked in a stain she'd tried to laugh off thirty seconds ago. Her face had gone past fear into a blank, animal terror, the look of someone realizing the ground she's standing on was never actually solid.

"What do you mean." Her voice cracked down the middle. "What — what are you saying, Simmy?"

For a moment nothing in the house moved except the crying.

Jessica came back out of the living room a different woman than the one who'd walked in — wide-eyed, open-handed — and when she reached the bottom of the stairs she didn't ask a single question. She just knelt, hard, knees cracking against the wood, and pulled all three of them into her at once, arms wide enough to make a fourth wall. Darrell's face mashed into her collarbone. Shayna's small fists twisting into her cardigan. Simone stood stiff inside the circle of her mother's arms; being held had become a fluency she'd lost.

"It's okay." Jessica said it into all of their hair at once, not because it was true but because it was the one sentence every mother is issued, standard equipment for exactly this collapse. "It's okay, it's okay — whatever it is. You three come first. You hear me? You come first."

She held them there until the crying softened into hiccups, until the part of her that had spent fifteen years reading scared kids across a desk came back online underneath the part of her that was still screaming somewhere inside her own chest. When she finally pulled back, it was her counselor's voice that answered, steady and procedural, the one she used on students who'd just told her something no kid should have to say out loud.

"Kitchen," she said. "All of us. Now."

She sat them at the table as though this were any other evening and set her hands flat on the wood — an old habit, needing the surface under her to hold still even when nothing else would.

"Have you called the police?"

Simone shook her head.

"So." Jessica's voice was very quiet. "What happened."

Two answers came at once, tumbling over each other.

"He fell!" Shayna said.

"I killed him," Simone said.

Jessica didn't look at Shayna at all. Her eyes locked onto Simone, the rest of the kitchen gone dark around the two of them.

"*What?*"

And Simone told her. All of it — the nights, how long it had been going on, what he'd said he'd do to Darrell and to Shayna if she ever made a sound about it — the sentences coming out flat and fast, ammunition she'd kept loaded for years and needed emptied before her body found a reason to stop. She told her about the living room, about Shayna on his lap, about the wink. Jessica turned to her younger daughter, and Shayna nodded, lip trembling, and started to cry again, the exhausted crying of a child who has just been asked to confirm something too big for her.

"You said you *moved* it," Jessica said. Her voice had gone strange, careful, the voice of someone testing ice. "With your — you said you looked at it."

She got up without waiting for an answer, opened the drawer, and set a spoon on the counter. Safer than a fork, some instinct told her, even now.

"Show me."

Simone stared at it. Nothing happened. She stared harder, jaw tight, and still the spoon sat there, ordinary, un-alive, a spoon.

Jessica's face changed — the fear curdling, fast, into something colder. "Tell me the truth, Simone." Her voice had an edge now that hadn't been there a minute ago. "Did you pick it up? Did you stab him yourself, with your hands, and now you're—" She couldn't finish it. *Making up a story so you don't have to carry what you actually did.*

"She's not lying." Shayna's voice cracked out of her, small and furious. "I *saw* it fly."

"Don't you lie to me, Simone." Jessica was standing over her now, hands trembling at her sides. "Don't you *dare* lie to me. Did you kill him? Did you stab him?"

Simone's eyes went past her mother, back to the spoon on the counter — and something in the shape of her mother's fear reached her where her own fear hadn't. She stopped trying. She let herself believe it, completely, with the unconsidered trust of a foot already committed to a step.

A lightness spread out from the center of her chest, and underneath it, impossibly, the exact weight of the spoon arrived — its temperature, the grain of the metal — a hand she didn't have, reaching across the room and closing around it anyway. She didn't tell it to fly. She simply stopped believing it couldn't.

The spoon lifted off the counter.

"*MOM! LOOK!*" Shayna's scream cracked through the kitchen.

Jessica spun around. The spoon hung above the counter, turning slightly, catching the overhead light. She crossed the kitchen, drawn toward something she both needed and dreaded to touch, and ran her hand beneath it, above it, searching for wire, for string, for any ordinary explanation her mind could survive.

There wasn't one.

She turned back to her daughter, and everything in her face had rearranged itself into something Simone had never seen on her mother before.

"Simone, baby." Her voice had gone almost to a whisper. "Come here."

---

The next half hour, Jessica took the shape Simone had already given her — Daryl, the toy, the fall — and built it out into something a stranger could believe. He'd tripped over a toy Darrell left on the floor while setting the table for dinner and gone down hard, catastrophically, onto a fork. The children had heard the crash and come running, Jessica added; they'd tried to lift him, shake him awake, get him to answer them — which was why there was blood on all three of them, not just on the one who'd actually been in the room. She walked each of them through it twice, smoothing a word here and there until it stopped sounding rehearsed — asked Shayna what she'd say if a policeman asked what she saw, asked Darrell to point at the floor and say *fell* — until the story settled into all three of them with the weight and shape of something true. Then she called 911.

The Newark detectives who responded worked the scene with a practiced blankness, men looking at something that technically added up and didn't feel right anyway — a woman's clean, consistent story; three children who all told it the same way; a dead man on the floor with a fork buried through his eye socket. They took photographs, took statements, took a look at the coffee table and the toy trucks staged a little too neatly by the wall, and left without anything they could point to as a lie.

It was two days later that the medical examiner's office called.

The wound didn't match a fall — the trajectory was wrong, the depth was wrong, the force required to drive a dinner fork through an orbital bone and into brain tissue was, by any calculation the ME's office could produce, beyond what a fall from standing height could generate. It also, they were careful to add, seemed beyond what any of the three children in that house could have produced on purpose. Which left, officially, nothing. An adult's strength, applied by no adult who'd been in the room. Or an accident that physics didn't support. The file went into a drawer that eventually gets reopened, by someone whose entire job is reopening exactly those drawers.

Jessica waited until the kids were finally asleep — Shayna curled into Darrell's side on Jessica's own bed, neither of them willing to be alone, Simone sitting upright against the headboard with her eyes open and dry, staring at nothing — before she went into the bathroom, ran the tap so the sound of it would cover her voice, and took a folded card out of the bottom of her jewelry box.

She hadn't looked at it in eleven years. The edges had softened to felt.

*Agent D. Whitfield*, it said, and a number with no area code she recognized as local anymore. He'd handed it to her the day two officers in dress uniform stood on her porch and told her, in the careful language the military keeps for exactly this purpose, that her husband was missing and presumed. Whitfield hadn't worn a uniform. He'd stood a little apart from the others, waited until they left, and told her — quietly, in something close to an apology — that if she ever needed anything, anything at all, she should call that number. She'd thanked him with the polite, distracted gratitude reserved for an umbrella she never expected to need, folded the card into the box, and forgotten it existed. Some part of her, some private, superstitious part, had kept it anyway. Just in case.

She didn't let herself think about why she was reaching for it now instead of 911 again, instead of a lawyer, instead of anyone else in her actual life. She told herself it was because he'd said *anything*. She told herself a lot of things, standing there with the tap running, before she dialed.

It rang twice. A woman picked up — not Whitfield's voice, younger, and if the number surprised her after eleven years of silence, nothing in her tone gave it away.

"This is Agent Reyes."

"I—" Jessica's voice came out smaller than she meant it to. "I have a card. Agent Whitfield gave it to me, a long time ago. He said to call if I ever needed help."

A pause, brief enough that she almost missed it. "Ma'am, can I get your name?"

"Jessica Floyd. My husband was — is — Airman Floyd. TACP. He's been missing since 2012."

Another pause, and this one she did notice, because it carried none of the texture of someone searching a database. It carried the texture of someone already holding the page open.

"Mrs. Floyd. Tell me what happened tonight."

She hadn't said anything had happened tonight.

She told her anyway — the fall, the fork, the ambulance, careful to keep her voice level, careful to keep it sounding rehearsed, the story she'd built with her children an hour ago, not the truth underneath it. She never mentioned Simone by name. She never said the word *power*, or *fork flying*, or anything that would have marked her as a woman needing commitment rather than help. She told herself, the whole time, that she was being careful. That she was protecting her daughter by saying less, not more.

When she finished, there was no surprise in the silence that followed. No questions about the impossible parts she hadn't mentioned. Just a beat of quiet — someone checking a name against a list.

"You did the right thing calling this number, Mrs. Floyd," Reyes said. "Don't talk to anyone else about tonight — not the police again, not family, not your pastor. Someone will reach out to you directly within twenty-four hours. Until then, keep your children close and keep this conversation between us."

"Is that — is everything going to be okay?"

"We'll take it from here." The line went dead before she could ask her anything else.

---

Reyes hung up and sat for a moment with her hand still on the phone before she picked it back up and dialed a different extension.

"Killiard."

"We've got a hit on a legacy flag. TACP program, 2012 cohort. Widow just called in an incident involving the daughter — she didn't say the word, but it's there. Local PD's already got a body and a medical examiner who can't explain the wound."

There was no surprise in Killiard's voice either, which was, by now, exactly what Reyes had learned to expect from her.

"Newark?"

"Newark."

"Get me the mother's statement, the ME's preliminary, and everything local PD has before they think to ask how a fork went through a grown man's skull as if his skull had been made of paper." A pause, the sound of something being written down. "And Reyes — nobody touches that ME's file. If it needs to disappear into a pending drawer for a while, make sure it does. Quietly."

She hung up without saying goodbye — she never did — and reached for her coat.

She had one arm through the sleeve when she stopped.

"Reyes. One more thing." She didn't turn back from the window. "Pull whatever we've already got on the stepfather. Full name's Daryl —" she read it off something on her desk Reyes couldn't see "— Daryl Floyd."

A pause on the line, a measuring silence, someone deciding whether the question was worth asking. Reyes asked it anyway. "We already have something on him?"

"We have something on a lot of people," Killiard said, which wasn't an answer, and both of them knew it wasn't. "Wherever it lives, leave it exactly where it is. I don't want it touched, I don't want it flagged, I don't want it showing up in anybody's search results for the next six months. I just want to know it's there."

"And if Newark PD gets curious about him? Priors, associates, anything —"

"They won't find anything to get curious about." Killiard finally turned from the window, coat half-on, and something in her voice had flattened into the register she used for repeating a rule rather than stating an opinion. "That's rather the point."

She finished pulling her coat on, picked up her phone, and was already three steps toward the door — toward whatever came next, toward Newark, toward a twelve-year-old girl unaware such a woman existed at all, let alone that she'd already been filed somewhere, waiting — when Reyes spoke again.

"Killiard. What are we actually looking at here?"

She didn't answer right away. When she did, it wasn't really an answer at all, just the same three words she'd used on the fork, on the girl, on every impossible thing that had crossed her desk in longer than she could easily remember.

"Let's find out," she said, and hung up.
